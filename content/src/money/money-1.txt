##### LESSON
id: money.budgeting.1
title: Where Does the Money Go?
minutes: 13
disclaimer: financial
check: In your own words: what is a budget really for, and why does “paying yourself first” work better than saving whatever is left at the end of the month?

=== hook
Many people earn a reasonable income, yet arrive at the end of each month wondering where it all went. They don’t remember buying anything big. There was no single disaster. The money simply leaked away, in small amounts, in dozens of directions.

Surveys in many countries find that a large share of households would struggle to cover an unexpected expense, like a medical bill or a broken phone, without borrowing. Often the problem isn’t income alone; it’s that money has no plan. A **budget** is that plan. Despite its reputation, a good budget isn’t about restriction. It’s about deciding in advance what your money is for.

=== core
A **budget** is simply a plan for your income: how much comes in, and where you intend it to go. It has three basic parts:

1. **Income**: what you actually receive after tax and deductions, sometimes called **take-home pay** or **net income**.
2. **Expenses**: what you spend. These come in two kinds:
   - **Fixed expenses**, which stay roughly the same each month: rent, loan repayments, phone plans, school fees, insurance.
   - **Variable expenses**, which change: food, transport, eating out, shopping, entertainment.
3. **Savings and goals**: money set aside for the future, from an emergency fund to long-term investments.

The basic rule is unglamorous but powerful: **spend less than you earn**, consistently. The gap between income and spending is what creates choices, security and wealth over time.

=== callout analogy
Think of your income as water flowing into a bucket with several holes. Some holes are necessary, like rent and food. Others are tiny leaks you barely notice: subscriptions, convenience deliveries, impulse purchases. A budget is the moment you look at the bucket carefully, decide which holes you want, and plug the ones you don’t.

=== text Step one: find out where it actually goes
Most people dramatically underestimate their spending on small, frequent items. Before making any plan, track your spending for a month. Bank and payment apps make this easier than ever, since most transactions, including UPI payments, card payments and transfers, are recorded automatically.

Sort your spending into categories and look for surprises. Common discoveries include forgotten **subscriptions**, frequent food delivery, and many small purchases that add up to a large monthly total. The goal isn’t guilt; it’s information. You can’t make good decisions about money you can’t see.

=== text A simple framework: 50/30/20
One popular starting point, described by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in a 2005 book, splits take-home pay into three parts:

- **About 50% for needs**: housing, food, utilities, transport, minimum loan payments, insurance.
- **About 30% for wants**: eating out, entertainment, hobbies, travel, non-essential shopping.
- **About 20% for savings and extra debt repayment.**

It’s a rule of thumb, not a law. In expensive cities, needs may take far more than half; on low incomes, saving 20% may be impossible at first. Many people adapt it, for example 60/20/20 or 70/20/10. The value is in having **some** deliberate split, rather than letting spending decide itself.

=== steps A worked monthly budget
- Suppose your take-home pay is **50,000** a month (in any currency).
- **Needs:** rent 15,000; groceries 6,000; transport 3,000; phone and internet 1,000; utilities 2,000. Total: **27,000** (54%).
- **Savings first:** you decide to set aside **10,000** (20%) the day your salary arrives: 5,000 to an emergency fund, 5,000 to a long-term investment.
- **Wants:** that leaves **13,000** (26%) for eating out, entertainment, clothes and everything else. You can spend this guilt-free, because your important goals are already covered.
- **Check monthly:** compare what you planned with what happened. If wants overshot, adjust next month rather than abandoning the plan.

=== text Pay yourself first
The most important budgeting habit is also the simplest: **save at the start of the month, not the end**.

If you plan to save “whatever is left over”, there usually isn’t much left, because spending tends to expand to fill the money available. Instead, move savings out of your spending account **automatically** on payday, through a standing instruction, a recurring deposit or an automatic investment plan. Then live on the rest.

This works because it uses the habit principles you met in the Mind region: it makes saving the **default**, requiring no willpower each month.

=== callout note | The emergency fund
Before investing for the long term, most financial educators recommend building an **emergency fund**: savings covering roughly **three to six months** of essential expenses, kept somewhere safe and easy to access, like a savings account. It protects you from being forced into expensive debt when something goes wrong, like job loss, illness or urgent repairs. Start small: even one month’s expenses is a meaningful cushion.

=== reveal Does cutting out small treats, like a daily coffee, really make you rich?
This idea, sometimes called the **“latte factor”**, has some truth: small daily spending adds up, and invested over decades it can grow substantially through compounding. But critics point out that for most people, the **big** decisions matter far more: housing costs, transport, debt and income growth. Cutting a daily coffee might save a few thousand a year; choosing a slightly cheaper home or avoiding a high-interest loan can save far more. The healthiest approach is usually to get the big things right first, then cut small spending you don’t actually value, while keeping the small pleasures you do.

=== keypoints
- A **budget** is a plan for income, covering **fixed** and **variable** expenses plus **savings**.
- The core rule: **spend less than you earn**, consistently.
- **Track spending** first; small, frequent purchases are easy to underestimate.
- The **50/30/20** rule (needs/wants/savings) is a flexible starting point, not a law.
- **Pay yourself first**: automate savings on payday, then spend what’s left.
- Build an **emergency fund** of roughly **3–6 months** of essential expenses.
- **Big decisions** (housing, transport, debt) usually matter more than small treats.

=== why
Budgeting is the foundation of every other money skill in this region. Without it, compounding has nothing to work with, debt creeps up unnoticed and emergencies become crises. With it, money becomes a tool for the things you care about, rather than a constant source of stress. Next, you’ll explore why spending decisions are often less rational than we think.

##### LESSON
id: money.budgeting.2
title: The Psychology of Spending
minutes: 14
disclaimer: financial
prereq: money.budgeting.1
check: In your own words: name two mental shortcuts that lead people to spend more than they intend, and explain how you could design your finances to work with your psychology rather than against it.

=== hook
In 2001, two professors at MIT, **Drazen Prelec** and **Duncan Simester**, ran an auction for tickets to sold-out basketball and baseball games. Half the bidders were told they must pay in **cash**; the other half by **credit card**. Same tickets, same people, same value.

The credit card group bid substantially more, in some cases more than **double** the cash group’s average bids. Nothing about the tickets had changed. Only the way the money felt leaving their hands. Much of our spending is driven not by careful calculation but by mental shortcuts, and businesses understand them very well.

=== core
Traditional economics often assumed people make rational decisions about money: weighing costs and benefits carefully and consistently. **Behavioural economics**, developed by psychologists like **Daniel Kahneman** and **Amos Tversky** and economists like **Richard Thaler** (Nobel Prize 2017), studies how people actually behave, and finds systematic, predictable departures from that ideal.

These aren’t signs of stupidity. They’re mental shortcuts that usually serve us well, but that can be exploited or can backfire with money. Knowing them is the first defence.

=== text Shortcut 1: the pain of paying
Paying with cash feels like a loss: you watch notes leave your wallet. Paying by card, phone or UPI feels almost painless, because the money is abstract and the moment of payment is quick. Researchers call this the **pain of paying**, and reducing it tends to increase spending.

This doesn’t mean digital payments are bad; they’re convenient and safe. But it means you may need **deliberate checks**, like reviewing spending weekly or setting app limits, to replace the natural brake that cash once provided.

=== text Shortcut 2: mental accounting
We treat money differently depending on where it came from or what we’ve labelled it for, even though money is interchangeable. Richard Thaler called this **mental accounting**.

People splurge a bonus, tax refund or gift money that they would never spend from their salary. Someone might keep savings earning little interest while carrying credit card debt costing far more, because the savings are mentally labelled “for emergencies”. Mental accounting can be useful too: labelling savings for specific goals, like “travel fund” or “emergency fund”, can make people less likely to raid them.

=== text Shortcut 3: present bias
We tend to value rewards **now** much more than rewards **later**, more than a consistent calculation would justify. This is called **present bias**. It’s why saving for retirement is so hard: the cost is today; the benefit is decades away, for a future self who feels almost like a stranger.

Present bias also explains the appeal of “buy now, pay later”, which lets you have the reward immediately while pushing the cost into the future.

=== callout analogy
Think of your present self and future self as two different people sharing a bank account. Present you is impulsive and loud; future you is quiet and not in the room. Every spending decision is made by present you. Good financial habits are ways of giving future you a vote, like automatic savings, which commit present you before temptation arrives.

=== text Shortcut 4: anchoring, decoys and “sales”
**Anchoring**: the first number we see shapes our judgement. A jacket “reduced from 8,000 to 4,000” feels like a bargain, even if 4,000 is what it was always worth. The original price is the anchor.

**The decoy effect**: in a famous example described by the behavioural economist Dan Ariely, a magazine offered three subscriptions: online only for 59, print only for 125, and print plus online for 125. Almost nobody chose print only, but its presence made print plus online look like a great deal, and many more people chose it than when only two options were offered. The middle option existed to steer choices.

**Scarcity and urgency**: “Only 2 left!” and countdown timers push quick decisions before you’ve had time to think.

=== text Shortcut 5: loss aversion and lifestyle creep
Kahneman and Tversky found that losses feel roughly **twice** as painful as equivalent gains feel good. This **loss aversion** makes free trials powerful: once you have something, cancelling it feels like a loss.

**Lifestyle inflation** (or lifestyle creep) is what happens when spending rises automatically with income. A pay rise brings a nicer phone, more expensive outings, a bigger flat. Each upgrade quickly feels normal, a process psychologists call **hedonic adaptation**, and the satisfaction fades while the costs remain.

=== steps Designing finances that work with your brain
- **Automate good decisions.** Automatic savings and investments on payday beat relying on willpower each month.
- **Use defaults.** When the UK began **automatically enrolling** workers into workplace pensions from 2012 (with the option to opt out), participation rose dramatically. Research by Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea in the US found a similar effect. You can apply the same idea to yourself.
- **Add friction to impulse spending.** Delete saved card details from shopping apps, unsubscribe from marketing emails, and use a waiting rule: for non-essential purchases above a set amount, wait 24 to 48 hours.
- **Save part of every raise.** When your income goes up, increase your automatic saving by part of the increase before you get used to spending it.
- **Label your savings** with specific goals to make them harder to raid.
- **Review regularly.** A weekly or monthly look at spending replaces the lost “pain of paying” with awareness.

=== reveal Does spending money on things or on experiences make people happier?
Research by psychologists including **Thomas Gilovich** suggests that, on average, **experiences** (trips, concerts, meals with friends) tend to bring more lasting happiness than material possessions, partly because we adapt to objects quickly, while experiences become memories and stories, and are often shared with others. Other studies, notably by **Elizabeth Dunn** and colleagues, found that spending money **on other people** tends to boost happiness more than spending it on yourself. These are averages, not rules, but they suggest that how you spend may matter as much as how much.

=== keypoints
- **Behavioural economics** studies predictable ways real people depart from purely rational money decisions.
- The **pain of paying** is reduced by cards and digital payments, which tends to raise spending.
- **Mental accounting**: we treat money differently depending on its source or label.
- **Present bias** makes future rewards feel less important than they should.
- **Anchoring**, **decoys**, **scarcity** cues and **loss aversion** are widely used in marketing.
- **Lifestyle inflation** absorbs pay rises as we adapt to new comforts.
- **Automation**, **defaults** and **friction** let you design finances that work with your psychology.

=== why
Every day, you face marketing, pricing and app designs built around these shortcuts. Understanding them won’t make you immune, since even experts fall for them, but it lets you notice the pull and build systems that protect your goals. These ideas also connect to the Philosophy region, where you’ll meet more cognitive biases that affect how we think about everything.

##### LESSON
id: money.compounding.2
title: Investing for the Long Game
minutes: 15
disclaimer: financial
prereq: money.compounding.1
check: In your own words: why do investors diversify, why do fees matter so much over the long term, and why do historical returns come with a warning that the future may be different?

=== hook
In 2007, the billionaire investor **Warren Buffett** made a famous bet. He wagered that over the next ten years, a simple, low-cost fund that just tracked the US stock market would beat a portfolio of hedge funds hand-picked by professional managers who charged high fees.

A firm called Protégé Partners took the bet. By the end of 2017, Buffett’s index fund had returned about **126%**, while the hedge funds averaged around **36%**. The professionals weren’t foolish; many were highly skilled. But their high fees and the difficulty of consistently beating the market proved too much. Buffett donated his winnings to charity.

=== core
In the previous lesson on compounding, you saw how money can grow over time when returns are reinvested. But savings accounts typically pay modest interest, which may barely keep pace with inflation. To grow wealth substantially over decades, many people **invest**: they put money into assets expected to grow faster, accepting more risk in return.

The main types of investment include:

- **Shares (stocks or equities):** small pieces of ownership in companies. If the company grows and profits, the shares may rise in value and pay **dividends**. If it struggles, they can fall, sometimes to zero.
- **Bonds:** loans to governments or companies, which pay interest and return the original amount at a set date. Usually less risky than shares, with lower expected returns.
- **Cash and deposits:** savings accounts and fixed deposits. Low risk, low return.
- **Property, gold and others:** real estate, precious metals and other assets, each with their own risks.

=== text Risk and return go together
A central principle of investing is that **higher expected returns usually come with higher risk**. Shares have historically outperformed bonds and cash over long periods, but with much bigger ups and downs along the way.

Long-run research covering many countries, such as studies by the economists Elroy Dimson, Paul Marsh and Mike Staunton, finds that global shares have returned roughly **5% a year above inflation** on average over more than a century. But that average hides brutal periods: during the 2008 global financial crisis, major stock markets fell by around **half** from their peaks. Some markets have taken over a decade to recover from major crashes. Investing in shares makes most sense for money you won’t need for many years.

=== text Don’t put all your eggs in one basket
**Diversification** means spreading your money across many different investments, so that a disaster in one doesn’t wipe you out.

If you own shares in just one company and it fails, you lose everything. If you own small amounts of hundreds of companies across different industries and countries, the failure of a few has a limited effect. Diversification reduces risk without necessarily reducing expected returns, which is why it’s sometimes called the only “free lunch” in investing.

=== callout analogy
Think of a diversified portfolio like a fruit stall that sells many kinds of fruit. If a pest destroys the mango harvest one year, the stall survives on bananas, apples and oranges. A stall that sells only mangoes is fine in a good year and ruined in a bad one.

=== text Index funds: owning the whole market
In **1976**, **John Bogle**, founder of the investment company Vanguard, launched one of the first **index funds** available to ordinary investors. Instead of paying managers to pick winning shares, an index fund simply buys all (or a representative sample) of the shares in a market index, such as the US S&P 500, India’s Nifty 50 or the UK’s FTSE 100.

Critics initially mocked it as “settling for average”. But decades of data have been striking. Reports comparing actively managed funds with their benchmark indices, such as S&P’s regular **SPIVA** reports, consistently find that a large majority of active funds underperform their benchmark over long periods, often **80–90%** over fifteen years in markets like the US. Results vary by market and period, but beating the market consistently, after fees, is very hard.

=== steps How fees eat your returns
- Suppose you invest **100,000** for **30 years**, earning **7%** a year before fees.
- **With fees of 0.1% a year** (typical of low-cost index funds), your effective return is 6.9%. After 30 years: about **740,000**.
- **With fees of 1.5% a year** (common for some actively managed funds or advisory arrangements), your effective return is 5.5%. After 30 years: about **498,000**.
- The difference, roughly **240,000**, went to fees, despite identical underlying returns.
- A fee of “just 1.5%” sounds small, but over three decades it consumed about a **third** of the final result. Compounding works on fees too.

=== text Time in the market
Trying to buy just before markets rise and sell just before they fall, called **market timing**, sounds sensible but is extremely difficult. Many of the market’s best days come shortly after its worst days, during periods of panic. Investors who sell in fear often miss the recovery.

A common alternative is to invest a fixed amount **regularly**, regardless of prices: in India often through a **SIP** (systematic investment plan). When prices are low, the same amount buys more units; when prices are high, it buys fewer. This doesn’t guarantee profits, but it removes the temptation to time the market and builds an automatic habit.

=== reveal If shares have done so well historically, why not put all your money in them?
Because **history is not a guarantee**, and your personal situation matters. Past returns came from specific historical conditions; future returns could be lower. Shares can fall sharply and stay down for years, which is disastrous if you need the money during that time, for a home deposit, school fees or retirement. That’s why people usually hold a mix of assets that suits their **time horizon** (when they’ll need the money) and **risk tolerance** (how much decline they can bear, financially and emotionally). Many also keep an emergency fund in safe, accessible savings. Choosing a mix for your own circumstances is where qualified, regulated advice can help.

=== callout caution | Beware promises of high, safe returns
Any investment promising **high returns with little or no risk** is a major warning sign. Higher returns require accepting more risk. Promises of guaranteed high returns are a hallmark of scams and Ponzi schemes, which you’ll study later in this region. In India, check that advisers and products are registered with **SEBI**; in the UK, with the **FCA**; in the US, with the **SEC** or **FINRA**.

=== keypoints
- Main asset types: **shares**, **bonds**, **cash** and others like property and gold.
- **Higher expected returns come with higher risk**; global shares returned about **5% a year above inflation** historically, with severe crashes.
- **Diversification** spreads risk across many investments.
- **Index funds** (pioneered by **John Bogle**, 1976) track a market cheaply; most active funds **underperform** their index over long periods.
- **Fees compound**: 1.5% vs 0.1% a year can cost around a **third** of the result over 30 years.
- **Time in the market** and regular investing (like **SIPs**) usually beat trying to time the market.
- Past returns are **not guaranteed**; match investments to your **time horizon** and **risk tolerance**.

=== why
Investing decisions you make early in life can shape your financial security for decades, thanks to compounding. Understanding risk, diversification and fees helps you avoid the most common and expensive mistakes, and recognise sales pitches that serve the seller more than you. This lesson gives general principles; your own decisions should reflect your circumstances and, where needed, regulated advice.

##### LESSON
id: money.debt.1
title: Borrowing: The Cost of Having It Now
minutes: 14
disclaimer: financial
check: In your own words: what does it really cost to borrow money, why can paying only the minimum on a credit card be so expensive, and how does a loan repayment schedule work?

=== hook
Imagine you owe **1,000** on a credit card charging **24%** a year in interest, and you decide to pay a fixed **30** a month until it’s gone. How long will it take?

Most people guess around three years. The real answer is about **four and a half years**, and by the end, you’ll have paid roughly **1,665** in total. Two-thirds of what you borrowed again, purely in interest. Borrowing isn’t inherently good or bad. But it always has a price, and that price is often far higher than it looks.

=== core
When you borrow money, you receive a sum now, called the **principal**, and agree to repay it later, plus **interest**: the price of using someone else’s money. Interest is usually expressed as a yearly percentage.

Common forms of debt include:

- **Credit cards**: flexible borrowing up to a limit, often with very high interest if not paid off in full each month.
- **Personal loans**: a fixed amount repaid in regular instalments.
- **Home loans (mortgages)**: large, long-term loans to buy property, secured against the home.
- **Vehicle and education loans.**
- **Buy now, pay later (BNPL)**: short-term instalment plans offered at checkout.

Debt can be **secured** (backed by an asset, like a house or car, that the lender can take if you don’t pay) or **unsecured** (backed only by your promise, like most credit cards). Unsecured debt is riskier for lenders, so it usually costs more.

=== text Reading the real price: APR
Lenders can quote costs in confusing ways: monthly rates, flat rates, processing fees, insurance add-ons. To compare loans fairly, look for the **annual percentage rate (APR)**, which includes interest plus most fees, expressed as a yearly rate. Many countries require lenders to disclose it.

Watch out for **flat interest rates**, sometimes used for personal or vehicle loans. A “10% flat” loan charges interest on the **original** amount for the whole term, even as you repay it, making the true cost much higher, often close to double the quoted figure. Always ask for the APR or the effective rate.

=== callout analogy
Think of borrowing as renting money. Just as renting a flat costs a monthly fee for the right to use someone else’s property, borrowing costs interest for the right to use someone else’s money. A good deal on rent can make sense; paying a fortune to rent something you could have waited to buy usually doesn’t.

=== steps The minimum payment trap, worked through
- You owe **1,000** at **24%** a year, which is about **2%** a month.
- In the first month, interest adds about **20**. You pay **30**, so only **10** actually reduces what you owe.
- Because most of each early payment goes to interest, the balance falls painfully slowly at first.
- Paying 30 a month, it takes about **56 months** to clear the debt, costing roughly **665** in interest.
- Double the payment to **60** a month and it takes about **20 months**, with total interest of roughly **230**. Paying more each month saves both time and a lot of money.
- Many credit cards set **minimum payments** as a small percentage of the balance, which falls as the balance falls, stretching repayment out for many years. Paying only the minimum is one of the most expensive habits in personal finance.

=== text How loan repayments work: amortisation
Most loans are repaid in equal regular instalments, in India often called **EMIs** (equated monthly instalments). Each payment covers that period’s interest, and the rest reduces the principal. This schedule is called **amortisation**.

Early in a long loan, most of each payment is **interest**, because the balance is large. Over time, the interest portion shrinks and more goes to principal. On a 20-year home loan, the early years may feel like you’re barely denting the balance. This is why making **extra payments early** can save a large amount of interest, and why the total paid over a long mortgage can be far more than the original loan.

=== text Can debt be good?
People often distinguish “good debt” from “bad debt”:

- Debt can make sense when it pays for something that **lasts longer than the loan** or **increases your future income**, at a reasonable interest rate: a home, education that leads to better earnings, or a business investment with solid prospects.
- Debt is usually harmful when it pays for **consumption** that’s gone long before the loan is repaid, like holidays, gadgets or daily spending, especially at high interest rates.

This distinction is helpful but not absolute. An education loan for a course with poor job prospects can be a burden; a home loan that stretches your budget to breaking point can be dangerous. The real questions are: **What does it cost? What does it buy? Can I comfortably afford the repayments, even if things go wrong?**

=== reveal Many credit cards charge no interest if you pay the full balance every month. So who pays for the rewards and cashback?
Partly the **shops**, which pay fees on each card transaction, often around 1–3% of the purchase, and often build that cost into prices for everyone, including people paying cash. And partly the **cardholders who don’t pay in full**, whose high interest charges are a major source of card company profits. People who pay in full every month can genuinely benefit from rewards. People who carry a balance usually pay far more in interest than they gain in rewards. A card is a useful tool if you treat it like a debit card; an expensive one if you treat it like extra income.

=== keypoints
- Borrowing means receiving **principal** now and repaying it with **interest**.
- Debt can be **secured** (backed by an asset) or **unsecured**; unsecured is usually more expensive.
- Compare loans using the **APR**, and beware **flat rates**, which understate the real cost.
- Paying only small or **minimum payments** on high-interest debt can take years and cost a large share of the original amount.
- **Amortisation**: early payments are mostly interest; **extra early payments** save the most.
- Debt can make sense for long-lasting assets at reasonable rates, but is risky for **consumption** at high rates.

=== why
Almost everyone borrows at some point, whether for education, a home, a vehicle or an emergency. Understanding the true cost of debt helps you borrow wisely when it makes sense and avoid the traps that keep many people stuck for years. Next, you’ll look at credit scores, the most dangerous forms of debt, and strategies for getting out.
