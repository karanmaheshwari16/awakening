##### LESSON
id: law.criminal-justice.2
title: Police, Rights and Reform
minutes: 15
disclaimer: legal
prereq: law.criminal-justice.1
check: In your own words: what rights do people have when arrested in India, what are Peel’s principles of policing, and what does evidence suggest about effective ways to reduce crime?

=== hook
In **1829**, the British Home Secretary **Robert Peel** established the **Metropolitan Police** in London, one of the first modern police forces. Officers, nicknamed “**bobbies**” or “peelers” after him, wore blue uniforms rather than military red, and were mostly unarmed, to distinguish them from soldiers.

A set of principles associated with Peel’s approach, though probably written down later by others, expressed a striking idea: “**the police are the public and the public are the police**”. Police, on this view, are simply citizens paid to give full-time attention to duties that every citizen has, and their power depends on public approval and cooperation, not force. Nearly two centuries later, the relationship between police and the public they serve remains one of the most important, and contested, questions in any society.

=== core
In the previous lesson, you saw how societies define crime and punishment. **Police** are usually the first point of contact between citizens and the criminal justice system: they prevent crime, respond to emergencies, investigate offences, arrest suspects and maintain public order.

Police hold unusual powers in a democracy: to stop, search, detain and, in extreme cases, use force. These powers are necessary to protect the public, but they can also be abused. Balancing **effective policing** with **protection of rights** is a central challenge for every justice system.

The **Peelian principles** include ideas such as:

- The basic mission of police is to **prevent crime and disorder**.
- Police depend on **public approval** and cooperation.
- Police should use **force only when necessary**, and only as much as needed.
- The test of police efficiency is the **absence** of crime and disorder, not visible police action against it.

=== text Your rights when arrested in India
India’s Constitution and laws give people important protections when arrested or detained:

- **Article 22** of the Constitution guarantees that an arrested person must be **informed of the grounds** for arrest as soon as possible, has the right to **consult and be defended by a lawyer** of their choice, and must be produced before the nearest **magistrate** within **24 hours** (excluding travel time).
- **Article 20(3)** protects against **self-incrimination**: no person accused of an offence can be compelled to be a witness against themselves.
- **Article 21** protects life and personal liberty, which courts have interpreted to include protection against custodial torture and the right to a fair trial.

In ***D. K. Basu v State of West Bengal*** (**1997**), the Supreme Court issued detailed **guidelines** to prevent custodial violence, including that police carrying out arrests must wear clear identification, prepare an **arrest memo** signed by a witness, inform a relative or friend of the arrest and place of detention, allow the arrested person to meet a lawyer during interrogation (though not throughout) and arrange medical examinations. Many of these requirements were later written into law.

=== callout analogy
Think of police powers like the powers of a skilled surgeon. A surgeon must be allowed to cut, because surgery saves lives. But strict rules govern when they can operate, how they must obtain consent, what records they must keep, and what happens if they cause unnecessary harm. Rights during arrest work like those surgical rules: they don’t stop police from doing their job; they make sure that their extraordinary powers are used carefully and accountably.

=== text Rights in other countries
Many democracies have similar protections:

- In the **United States**, the Supreme Court’s ruling in ***Miranda v Arizona*** (**1966**) requires police to warn suspects in custody, before questioning, that they have the **right to remain silent**, that anything they say can be used against them, and that they have the **right to a lawyer**. These became known as **Miranda rights**.
- In **England and Wales**, the **Police and Criminal Evidence Act** of **1984** set rules for stop and search, arrest, detention and interrogation, including the recording of interviews.

=== text Custodial violence and police reform
**Custodial violence**, abuse or torture of people in police or judicial custody, is a serious concern in many countries, including India. Human rights organisations and India’s National Human Rights Commission record deaths in custody every year. Causes are often linked to pressure to solve cases quickly, reliance on confessions, inadequate training and weak accountability.

India’s Supreme Court addressed police reform in ***Prakash Singh v Union of India*** (**2006**), directing states to take steps such as:

- Setting up **state security commissions** to reduce political interference in policing.
- Ensuring **fixed tenures** for senior police officers, so they can’t be transferred arbitrarily.
- **Separating** investigation from law-and-order duties, so investigators can focus on solving crimes.
- Creating **police complaints authorities** to investigate serious misconduct.

Implementation of these directions has been uneven across states, and police reform remains an ongoing challenge. Other issues include shortages of police personnel, poor working conditions and long hours, and the need for better forensic and investigative capacity.

=== steps Bail: liberty before trial
- **Bail** is the temporary release of an accused person while awaiting trial, often on conditions such as a promise to appear in court, a financial bond or restrictions on travel.
- In a famous **1977** judgment, Justice **V. R. Krishna Iyer** of the Supreme Court declared that the basic rule is “**bail, not jail**”, reflecting the presumption of innocence.
- Courts consider factors like the seriousness of the offence, the risk that the accused might flee, tamper with evidence or influence witnesses, and their criminal history.
- Some special laws, such as certain anti-terrorism and anti-money-laundering laws, make bail much harder to obtain, which supporters say is necessary for serious threats and critics say leads to long detention without trial.
- Poverty is a major barrier: many undertrials remain in jail simply because they can’t afford bail bonds, which is why courts and legal aid authorities have pushed for release on personal bonds for poor accused people.

=== text Young people and the law
Most legal systems treat children differently from adults, recognising that young people are still developing, more capable of change and more vulnerable. In India, the **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act** of **2015** governs children in conflict with the law, defining a child as anyone **under 18**. Children are generally dealt with by **Juvenile Justice Boards** rather than regular criminal courts, with an emphasis on rehabilitation, and can’t be sentenced to death or life imprisonment without the possibility of release.

After the brutal **2012** gang rape and murder of a young woman in Delhi, which provoked nationwide protests and led to major changes in laws on sexual violence, the 2015 Act introduced a provision allowing **16- to 18-year-olds** accused of **heinous** offences to be tried as adults, after an assessment of their mental capacity and maturity. Supporters saw it as necessary for justice in the most serious cases; child rights advocates argued it went against the principle of rehabilitation and evidence on adolescent development.

=== reveal What does research suggest actually reduces crime?
Researchers have studied many approaches, with some fairly consistent findings. **Hot spots policing**, focusing police resources on the small number of locations where much crime is concentrated, has been found in many studies to reduce crime in those places, without simply displacing it elsewhere. **Focused deterrence**, which identifies the small number of people driving serious violence, offers them support alongside clear warnings, and works with communities, has shown promising results in some cities. **Problem-oriented policing**, which tackles the underlying conditions behind recurring problems, also has good support. Beyond policing, factors like **street lighting**, **youth programmes**, **education**, **employment** and **reducing lead exposure** (a much-debated hypothesis) have been linked to lower crime. **Trust** between police and communities matters too: people are more likely to report crimes and cooperate with investigations when they see police as fair and legitimate, echoing Peel’s principles.

=== keypoints
- Modern policing began with **Peel’s Metropolitan Police (1829)**; **Peelian principles** stress prevention, public consent and minimal force.
- In India, **Article 22** guarantees being informed of grounds for arrest, access to a lawyer and production before a magistrate within **24 hours**; **Article 20(3)** protects against **self-incrimination**.
- ***D. K. Basu* (1997)** set guidelines against custodial violence; ***Prakash Singh* (2006)** directed police reforms, unevenly implemented.
- The US requires **Miranda warnings** (**1966**); England uses **PACE (1984)**.
- “**Bail, not jail**” (Krishna Iyer, **1977**), but poverty keeps many undertrials in prison.
- India’s **Juvenile Justice Act (2015)** treats under-18s differently, but allows 16–18-year-olds to be tried as adults for heinous crimes.
- **Hot spots policing**, **focused deterrence** and **community trust** are supported by research as ways to reduce crime.

=== why
Your interactions with police, and the rights you have during them, are some of the most direct ways you encounter state power. Knowing your rights, understanding the debates about police reform, and seeing what evidence suggests works helps you engage as an informed citizen in making policing both effective and fair.

##### LESSON
id: law.contracts.1
title: Contracts: Promises the Law Will Enforce
minutes: 14
disclaimer: legal
check: In your own words: what are the essential elements of a valid contract under Indian law, what did the Carbolic Smoke Ball case decide, and what remedies are available if a contract is broken?

=== hook
In **1891**, a London company called the **Carbolic Smoke Ball Company** advertised a remarkable product: a rubber ball containing carbolic acid, with a tube for inhaling its vapours, which it claimed could prevent influenza. The advertisement promised to pay **£100**, a large sum at the time, to anyone who used the smoke ball as directed and still caught flu. To show its sincerity, it said it had deposited £1,000 in a bank for the purpose.

A woman named **Louisa Carlill** bought one, used it faithfully three times a day for weeks, and then caught influenza. She claimed her £100. The company refused, arguing that its advertisement was mere sales talk, not a serious promise. In **1893**, the English Court of Appeal ruled in her favour: the advertisement was a genuine offer, which she had accepted by using the product as instructed. ***Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company*** is still taught to law students around the world, including in India, as a classic case about when promises become legally binding.

=== core
A **contract** is an agreement between two or more parties that the law will **enforce**. Not every promise is a contract: if a friend promises to help you move house and doesn’t turn up, you can’t usually take them to court. Contracts are agreements made with the intention of creating legal obligations.

Contracts are everywhere in daily life, often without us noticing:

- Buying groceries, a bus ticket or a cinema ticket.
- Accepting a job.
- Renting a flat.
- Signing up for a mobile phone plan or an online service.
- Taking out a loan or buying insurance.

In India, contracts are governed mainly by the **Indian Contract Act** of **1872**, one of the oldest laws still in force in the country, drafted during British rule and based on English common law principles.

=== text The essential elements of a valid contract
Under the Indian Contract Act, an agreement is a valid contract if it’s made with:

1. **Offer and acceptance**: one party makes a clear **offer** (a proposal), and the other **accepts** it unconditionally. The acceptance must match the offer; changing the terms creates a **counter-offer** instead.
2. **Lawful consideration**: something of value is exchanged, such as money, goods, services or a promise to do or not do something. In everyday terms: each side gives and gets something.
3. **Capacity**: the parties must be legally **competent** to contract: adults (18 or over in India), of sound mind and not disqualified by law.
4. **Free consent**: agreement must be given freely, without **coercion**, **undue influence**, **fraud**, **misrepresentation** or certain **mistakes**.
5. **Lawful object**: the purpose of the contract must be legal. An agreement to commit a crime, or one that is fraudulent or against public policy, is **void**.
6. **Intention to create legal relations**: the parties must intend the agreement to be legally binding. In business deals, this is usually presumed; in family or social arrangements, it usually isn’t.

=== callout analogy
Think of a contract like a recipe that only works if all the essential ingredients are present. Offer and acceptance are the flour and water; consideration is the yeast; capacity, free consent and a lawful object are the oven conditions. Leave out a crucial ingredient, or bake it in the wrong conditions, and what you get isn’t a valid contract, however official it looks on paper.

=== text Offers, invitations and acceptance
An important distinction is between an **offer** and an **invitation to treat** (an invitation to make offers):

- Goods displayed in a shop window or on a supermarket shelf, with price tags, are usually treated as **invitations to treat**: the customer makes the offer at the checkout, which the shop can accept or refuse.
- Advertisements are usually invitations to treat too, but, as the **Carbolic Smoke Ball** case showed, an advertisement can be a genuine **offer** if it’s clear, definite and shows an intention to be bound, especially where it promises a reward to anyone who performs certain acts. This is called a **unilateral contract**: anyone who performs the act accepts the offer.

Acceptance usually has to be **communicated** to the person making the offer, though, in unilateral contracts like rewards, performing the act is enough.

=== text Who can make contracts?
Some people lack the legal capacity to make binding contracts:

- **Minors**: in the landmark case ***Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose*** (**1903**), decided by the Privy Council on appeal from Calcutta, a teenager had mortgaged his property to a moneylender. The court ruled that a contract with a **minor** is **void from the beginning**: it has no legal effect at all, protecting young people from exploitation.
- **People of unsound mind** at the time of contracting, unable to understand the contract and its effects.
- **People disqualified by law**, in specific circumstances.

=== steps When a contract is broken: remedies
- A **breach of contract** happens when one party fails to perform their obligations, performs them badly or makes it clear they won’t perform.
- The most common remedy is **damages**: money to compensate the injured party, aiming to put them in the position they would have been in if the contract had been performed.
- Damages generally cover losses that were **reasonably foreseeable** when the contract was made. This principle comes from a famous English case, ***Hadley v Baxendale*** (**1854**), in which a mill owner couldn’t recover profits lost because a carrier delayed delivering a broken crankshaft, since the carrier couldn’t have known the mill would stop entirely without it. India’s Contract Act adopts a similar principle.
- **Specific performance**: in some cases, especially involving unique items like land or rare goods, courts can order the party to actually **carry out** the contract, rather than just pay money. Amendments to India’s Specific Relief Act in **2018** made specific performance more widely available.
- **Injunctions**: court orders preventing a party from doing something, such as breaching a confidentiality agreement.
- **Rescission**: cancelling the contract and restoring both parties to their original positions, for instance where consent was obtained by fraud.

=== reveal Do contracts have to be in writing?
Not always. Under Indian law, many contracts can be made **orally** or even through **conduct**: when you buy vegetables at a market, no written contract is needed. However, some contracts must be in **writing** and sometimes **registered** to be valid or enforceable, such as contracts for the sale of land and long-term property leases, as required by laws like the Transfer of Property Act and the Registration Act. Even when not required, having a contract **in writing** is usually wise, because it provides clear evidence of what was agreed, reducing disputes. Today, **electronic contracts**, formed by clicking “I agree” or signing digitally, are recognised under India’s **Information Technology Act** of **2000**. The next lesson explores the fine print in these everyday digital agreements.

=== keypoints
- A **contract** is an agreement the law will enforce; in India, contracts are governed mainly by the **Indian Contract Act (1872)**.
- Essential elements: **offer and acceptance**, **lawful consideration**, **capacity**, **free consent**, **lawful object** and **intention to create legal relations**.
- Shop displays and most adverts are **invitations to treat**, but ***Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* (1893)** showed an advert can be a binding **offer** (a **unilateral contract**).
- ***Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose* (1903)**: contracts with **minors** are **void**.
- Remedies for breach include **damages** (limited to **foreseeable** losses, from ***Hadley v Baxendale*, 1854**), **specific performance**, **injunctions** and **rescission**.
- Many contracts can be oral, but some, like **land sales**, must be **written and registered**; **electronic contracts** are valid under the **IT Act (2000)**.

=== why
You enter contracts almost every day, from buying a ticket to signing up for an app or accepting a job. Understanding what makes a contract valid and what happens when one is broken helps you protect yourself, avoid disputes and know when an agreement is legally binding. The final lesson looks at consumer rights and the fine print of modern agreements.

##### LESSON
id: law.contracts.2
title: Fine Print, Consumer Rights and Digital Agreements
minutes: 15
disclaimer: legal
prereq: law.contracts.1
check: In your own words: why do standard form contracts raise concerns, what protections does India’s Consumer Protection Act provide, and what are dark patterns?

=== hook
In **2016**, two researchers, **Jonathan Obar** and **Anne Oeldorf-Hirsch**, created a fake social networking site and asked over 500 university students to sign up. The site’s terms of service included some unusual clauses: users agreed that their data could be shared with the US National Security Agency and with employers, and, buried in the text, that they would give up their **first-born child** as payment for the service.

About **98%** of participants agreed without noticing the clauses. Most spent less than a minute on terms that would take an average reader around half an hour or more to read properly. The experiment highlighted something almost everyone does every day: agreeing to contracts we haven’t read, written by companies far more powerful than us. It raises important questions about fairness, consent and how the law should protect consumers.

=== core
In the previous lesson, you saw the classic idea of a contract: two parties freely negotiating and agreeing on terms. But many modern contracts don’t work that way. When you sign up for a mobile phone plan, open a bank account, buy insurance, book a flight or install an app, you’re usually presented with a long **standard form contract**, written in advance by the company, which you can only accept or reject as a whole. There’s no negotiation. These are sometimes called **contracts of adhesion**.

Standard form contracts are efficient: companies can’t negotiate separately with millions of customers. But they raise concerns:

- **Unequal bargaining power**: the company writes all the terms; the consumer has little choice but to accept.
- **Information asymmetry**: terms may be long, complex and full of legal jargon, and few people read them.
- **Unfair terms**: contracts may include clauses that heavily favour the company, such as limiting its liability, allowing it to change terms unilaterally or imposing harsh penalties on customers.

=== text Unfair terms and how law responds
Courts and legislatures have developed ways to protect weaker parties:

- Courts can refuse to enforce terms that are **unconscionable**, grossly unfair, or **against public policy**.
- Ambiguous terms in standard form contracts are often interpreted **against** the party that wrote them.
- Many countries have laws specifically targeting **unfair contract terms** in consumer contracts. India’s **Consumer Protection Act** of **2019** defines **unfair contracts** and allows consumer commissions to declare such terms void.
- In a **2019** case, India’s Supreme Court held that a builder could not enforce one-sided terms in an apartment purchase agreement that heavily favoured the builder, finding them unfair to homebuyers.

=== callout analogy
Think of a standard form contract like a restaurant with a fixed menu and no substitutions. That’s convenient and efficient, and usually fine. But imagine if the menu contained, in tiny print, a rule that you must pay double if you leave any food, and that the restaurant isn’t responsible if the food makes you ill. You’d expect someone, a regulator or a court, to step in, because you had no real chance to negotiate. Consumer protection law is that someone.

=== text India’s Consumer Protection Act
The **Consumer Protection Act, 2019**, replaced an earlier 1986 law, updating consumer protection for the age of e-commerce. Key features include:

- **Consumer rights**: including the right to be protected against hazardous goods and services, the right to be **informed** about quality, quantity, purity and price, the right to **choose**, the right to be **heard**, the right to seek **redressal** and the right to **consumer education**.
- **Consumer commissions** at **district**, **state** and **national** levels, which hear complaints about defective goods, deficient services and unfair trade practices. They’re designed to be simpler, cheaper and faster than ordinary courts, and consumers can file complaints themselves, including **online** through the **e-Daakhil** portal.
- **Product liability**: manufacturers, sellers and service providers can be held liable for harm caused by defective products.
- **Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA)**: a regulator that can investigate violations of consumer rights, order recalls, and act against **misleading advertisements**, including penalties for endorsers, such as celebrities, who promote misleading ads without due diligence.
- **E-commerce rules**: obligations on online marketplaces to display information about sellers, prices, return policies and grievance redressal.

=== text Dark patterns
**Dark patterns** are design tricks in websites and apps that manipulate users into doing things they didn’t intend, such as buying more, sharing more data or finding it hard to cancel. They exploit the psychological shortcuts you met in the Money and Philosophy regions.

Common examples include:

- **False urgency**: fake countdown timers or “only 2 left!” messages.
- **Basket sneaking**: adding items or charges to your cart without clear consent.
- **Confirm shaming**: making the option to decline embarrassing, like “No thanks, I don’t want to save money”.
- **Forced continuity**: making it easy to start a free trial but hard to cancel before being charged.
- **Hidden costs**: revealing extra fees only at the final step.
- **Disguised advertisements** presented as genuine content.

In **2023**, India’s Central Consumer Protection Authority issued guidelines identifying and prohibiting **13** types of dark patterns on online platforms, joining regulators in the European Union and the United States in tackling such practices.

=== steps Practical tips before you agree
- **Skim for the key terms**, even if you don’t read everything: price, renewal and cancellation terms, fees and penalties, data sharing, and how disputes are resolved.
- **Watch for automatic renewals**, and set reminders to cancel free trials if you don’t want them to continue.
- **Keep records**: save receipts, screenshots of offers and copies of contracts and terms.
- **Check privacy settings**, and be cautious about granting apps unnecessary permissions.
- **For big decisions**, such as buying property, taking a loan, signing an employment contract or a rental agreement, read carefully and consider getting independent legal advice. India’s **Model Tenancy Act** of **2021** provides a framework that states can adopt to make rental agreements clearer and fairer for landlords and tenants.
- **Know where to complain**: start with the company’s grievance officer, then use consumer helplines (India’s **National Consumer Helpline**) and consumer commissions if needed.

=== text Arbitration and dispute resolution clauses
Many contracts include an **arbitration clause**, requiring disputes to be resolved by a private arbitrator rather than in court. Arbitration can be faster, more flexible and confidential, and is widely used in business. India’s **Arbitration and Conciliation Act** of **1996** governs it.

But in consumer contracts, arbitration clauses can disadvantage consumers if they’re forced to use costly or distant arbitration. India’s Supreme Court has held that consumers can still approach **consumer commissions** despite arbitration clauses in their contracts, protecting their access to the simpler consumer remedies.

=== reveal What are smart contracts, and are they really contracts?
**Smart contracts** are computer programs stored on a **blockchain**, a shared digital ledger used by cryptocurrencies, that automatically execute actions when certain conditions are met: for example, automatically releasing payment when a delivery is confirmed. Supporters say they can reduce the need for intermediaries, cut costs and ensure agreements are carried out exactly as coded. But there are important caveats. Code can contain **bugs**, as you saw in the Technology region, and once executed on a blockchain, transactions may be impossible to reverse. Smart contracts can’t easily handle situations requiring human judgement, such as whether goods were of acceptable quality, and their legal status varies between countries. Whether a smart contract is legally enforceable depends on whether it meets the ordinary requirements for a contract, like offer, acceptance, consideration and capacity. In practice, code alone rarely replaces the need for legal agreements and human judgement.

=== keypoints
- Many modern contracts are **standard form contracts** (contracts of **adhesion**), raising concerns about **unequal bargaining power**, **information asymmetry** and **unfair terms**.
- In a **2016** experiment, about **98%** of people agreed to terms of service without noticing a clause giving up their first-born child.
- Courts can refuse **unconscionable** terms; India’s **Consumer Protection Act (2019)** allows unfair contract terms to be declared void.
- The Act provides **consumer rights**, **consumer commissions** (with online filing via **e-Daakhil**), **product liability** and the **CCPA** regulator, including action on **misleading ads**.
- **Dark patterns** manipulate users; India banned **13** types in **2023**.
- Read key terms, watch for **auto-renewals**, keep records and know how to complain.
- Consumers can approach **consumer commissions** despite **arbitration clauses**; **smart contracts** have limits and uncertain legal status.

=== why
Every day, you agree to contracts you haven’t negotiated and often haven’t read. Understanding standard form contracts, consumer rights and manipulative design helps you protect your money, your data and your rights, and use the legal remedies available when businesses treat you unfairly. It’s a practical, everyday application of everything this region has explored: law as a tool to balance power and protect people.
