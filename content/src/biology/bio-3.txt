##### LESSON
id: biology.microbes.2
title: Antibiotics, Resistance and Pandemics
minutes: 15
disclaimer: health
prereq: biology.microbes.1
check: In your own words: how does antibiotic resistance evolve, why does misuse of antibiotics make it worse, and what makes a new disease likely to become a pandemic?

=== hook
In **September 1928**, the Scottish scientist **Alexander Fleming** returned to his London laboratory after a summer holiday and noticed something odd about a dish of bacteria he had left out. A mould had contaminated it, and around the mould was a clear ring where the bacteria had died.

Fleming identified the mould as *Penicillium* and named the bacteria-killing substance it produced **penicillin**. Little came of it for over a decade, until **Howard Florey**, **Ernst Chain** and colleagues at Oxford found ways to purify and produce it in the early 1940s, just in time to save many lives in World War II. All three shared the 1945 Nobel Prize. In his acceptance speech, Fleming gave a warning: misuse of penicillin could make bacteria resistant to it. He was right.

=== core
**Antibiotics** are medicines that kill bacteria or stop them from multiplying, by targeting structures and processes that bacteria have but human cells don’t, such as bacterial cell walls or bacterial ribosomes. Before antibiotics, a scratch that became infected, pneumonia or a difficult childbirth could easily be fatal. Antibiotics transformed medicine, making surgery, cancer treatment, organ transplants and care for premature babies far safer.

But bacteria evolve. **Antibiotic resistance** happens when bacteria change so that antibiotics no longer work against them. It’s natural selection in action, the same process you met in the evolution lessons, happening fast because bacteria reproduce rapidly, sometimes dividing every 20 minutes.

=== steps How resistance evolves
- In a large population of bacteria, random **mutations** mean a few individuals may happen to be slightly resistant to an antibiotic.
- When the antibiotic is used, it kills most of the bacteria, but the resistant ones are more likely to survive.
- The survivors multiply, and the next generation contains more resistant bacteria.
- Repeated exposure **selects** for ever more resistant strains.
- Bacteria can also share resistance genes directly with each other, even across different species, by swapping small loops of DNA called **plasmids**, spreading resistance quickly.
- The antibiotic doesn’t create resistance; it **selects** for resistant bacteria that already exist, by removing their competitors.

=== callout analogy
Imagine spraying a field of weeds with a weedkiller that kills 99.9% of them. The few survivors, which happened to be naturally tough, now have the whole field to themselves, and their seeds spread everywhere. Next season, the same weedkiller barely works. Each time you spray, you’re clearing space for the toughest survivors. Overusing antibiotics does the same with bacteria.

=== text Why misuse makes it worse
Every use of antibiotics creates selection pressure, so unnecessary use speeds up resistance:

- **Using antibiotics for viral infections**, like colds and most sore throats and flu, which antibiotics can’t treat at all.
- **Not completing courses as prescribed**, or using leftover antibiotics without medical advice. (Advice on course length is evolving; follow your doctor’s guidance.)
- **Over-the-counter sales without prescription**, common in many countries, including parts of India, though rules have tightened for some antibiotics.
- **Agriculture**: in many countries, large amounts of antibiotics are given to livestock, sometimes to promote growth rather than treat disease. Resistant bacteria can spread from farms to people through food, water and the environment. Several countries, including the EU, have banned antibiotics as growth promoters.
- **Pollution**: antibiotics released from factories, hospitals and sewage into rivers and soil can encourage resistance in environmental bacteria.

=== text A growing crisis
A major study published in *The Lancet* in **2022** estimated that in **2019**, bacterial antimicrobial resistance directly caused about **1.27 million** deaths worldwide and was associated with nearly **5 million**. That’s more direct deaths than HIV/AIDS or malaria caused that year. Some infections, like certain forms of tuberculosis and gonorrhoea, have become very difficult to treat.

At the same time, few new classes of antibiotics have been developed in recent decades, partly because developing them is expensive and they’re used sparingly to preserve their effectiveness, making them less profitable. Responses include stewardship programmes in hospitals to use antibiotics more carefully, better infection control, vaccines to prevent infections in the first place, faster diagnostic tests, and new incentives for developing antibiotics. India’s national action plan and the “Red Line” campaign, which marks prescription-only antibiotic packaging with a red vertical line, aim to reduce misuse.

=== text Pandemics: when diseases go global
An **epidemic** is a rapid increase in cases of a disease in a population; a **pandemic** is an epidemic that spreads across many countries or continents.

Most new infectious diseases are **zoonotic**: they jump from animals to humans, as you saw in the farming lessons. HIV came from chimpanzees and other primates; influenza often from birds and pigs; Ebola likely from bats. Factors increasing the risk of new pandemics include:

- **Close contact with animals**, through farming, wildlife trade and habitat destruction, which brings people into contact with new viruses.
- **Dense cities** where diseases spread easily.
- **Global travel**, which can carry an infection around the world within days.

=== steps What makes a disease spread
- Epidemiologists use a number called **R₀** (“R nought”): the average number of people one infected person infects in a population with no immunity.
- If R₀ is above **1**, cases grow; below 1, the outbreak shrinks.
- Measles has a very high R₀, often estimated around **12 to 18**; seasonal flu is much lower, around 1 to 2. The original strain of the virus that causes COVID-19 was estimated at around 2 to 3; later variants were more transmissible.
- Spread also depends on how the disease is transmitted (air, droplets, touch, water, insects), how long people are infectious, whether people can spread it before showing symptoms and how deadly it is.
- Measures like vaccination, masks, ventilation, handwashing, testing, isolation and reducing contacts aim to push the effective reproduction number below 1.

=== text COVID-19
The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, was first identified in Wuhan, China, in late **2019**. The WHO declared it a pandemic in **March 2020**. The World Health Organization estimated around **15 million** excess deaths globally in 2020 and 2021, far more than officially reported COVID deaths, and India was among the countries with the largest estimated excess mortality, a figure the Indian government disputed.

The pandemic showed both the power of science, with effective vaccines developed in under a year, and the challenges of global coordination, misinformation and unequal access to vaccines. The exact origin of the virus, whether through natural spillover from animals or a laboratory-related incident, remains debated and unresolved.

=== reveal What is “One Health”, and why do experts say it’s essential for preventing future pandemics?
**One Health** is an approach recognising that the health of **people**, **animals** and the **environment** are deeply connected. Many threats, such as zoonotic diseases, antibiotic resistance and food safety, cross between them. Preventing pandemics means monitoring diseases in wildlife and livestock, regulating wildlife trade, reducing deforestation that brings people into contact with new pathogens, using antibiotics carefully in both medicine and farming, and sharing information across borders quickly. It requires doctors, vets, ecologists and governments to work together. In **2025**, WHO member states adopted a **Pandemic Agreement** aimed at improving global preparedness and more equitable sharing of vaccines and medicines, though how it will work in practice is still being developed.

=== keypoints
- **Fleming** discovered **penicillin** in **1928**; Florey and Chain made it a usable medicine in the 1940s.
- **Antibiotic resistance** evolves by **natural selection**; bacteria also share resistance genes via **plasmids**.
- Misuse, including antibiotics for **viral infections**, **over-the-counter** sales and use in **livestock**, speeds up resistance.
- Resistance directly caused about **1.27 million** deaths in **2019**; few new antibiotic classes are being developed.
- Most new diseases are **zoonotic**; **R₀** measures how fast a disease spreads.
- **COVID-19** caused an estimated **15 million** excess deaths in 2020–21; its origin remains unresolved.
- **One Health** links human, animal and environmental health to prevent future crises.

=== why
Antibiotic resistance and pandemics are among the greatest threats to global health, and both are shaped by human behaviour. Simple actions, like not demanding antibiotics for viral illnesses, completing treatments as advised, getting vaccinated and supporting good public health, make a real difference. Understanding the biology helps you see why.

##### LESSON
id: biology.ecosystems.1
title: How Ecosystems Work
minutes: 15
check: In your own words: how does energy flow through a food web, why are there usually far fewer top predators than plants, and what is a keystone species?

=== hook
In **1995**, after an absence of about 70 years, grey wolves were reintroduced into **Yellowstone National Park** in the United States. Wolves had been hunted out of the park by the 1920s. Without them, elk populations had grown large, browsing heavily on young trees along rivers.

After the wolves returned, elk numbers fell and their behaviour changed. Many scientists reported that in some areas, willows and aspens began to recover, providing habitat for birds and beavers. The story of wolves transforming Yellowstone became famous, though later research suggests the effects were real but more complex and patchy than the most popular versions claim, with other factors like bears, hunters and drought also playing a role. Either way, it showed how one species can ripple through an entire ecosystem.

=== core
An **ecosystem** is a community of living things interacting with each other and with their physical environment, such as soil, water, air and climate. Ecosystems range from a small pond to a rainforest, a coral reef or a desert.

Every ecosystem depends on two fundamental processes:

1. **Energy flows** through it, entering mostly as sunlight and leaving as heat.
2. **Nutrients cycle** within it, being reused again and again.

Living things in an ecosystem can be grouped by how they get energy:

- **Producers**: mainly plants, algae and some bacteria, which make their own food through photosynthesis, as you saw in the cells lessons.
- **Consumers**: animals that eat other organisms. **Herbivores** eat plants; **carnivores** eat animals; **omnivores** eat both.
- **Decomposers**: bacteria, fungi and other organisms that break down dead material and waste, returning nutrients to the soil.

=== text Food chains and food webs
A **food chain** shows a simple sequence of who eats whom: grass → deer → tiger. Each step is called a **trophic level**.

Real ecosystems are far more complex, with many interconnected food chains forming a **food web**. Deer eat many plants; tigers eat deer, wild boar and other prey; birds, insects, fungi and microbes all play their part. Food webs show how a change to one species can affect many others.

=== text The ten percent rule
Energy is lost at every step up a food chain. When a deer eats grass, most of the energy in the grass doesn’t end up as deer: much is used for the deer’s movement, warmth and life processes, released as heat, as you saw in the Physics region, or lost in waste.

On average, only about **10%** of the energy at one trophic level is passed on to the next, a rough rule of thumb (the real figure varies, often from around 5% to 20%). So:

- If plants capture **10,000** units of energy, herbivores might get about **1,000**.
- Carnivores eating those herbivores might get about **100**.
- Top predators might get about **10**.

This is why ecosystems usually have far more plants than herbivores, and far fewer top predators, forming an **energy pyramid**. It’s also why food chains rarely have more than four or five levels: there isn’t enough energy left at the top.

=== callout analogy
Think of energy in a food chain like money passed through a chain of middlemen who each spend 90% on their own running costs. Start with 10,000 rupees at the bottom, and by the fourth person, only 10 rupees remain. That’s why a forest can feed thousands of deer but only a handful of tigers.

=== text Nutrient cycles
Unlike energy, which flows through ecosystems, **nutrients** like carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus are **recycled**.

- **The carbon cycle**: plants take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis and build it into sugars. Animals eat plants, and both release carbon dioxide through respiration. Decomposers release carbon from dead material. Over millions of years, some carbon becomes buried as fossil fuels, which humans now release by burning, as you’ll see in the Earth & Climate region.
- **The nitrogen cycle**: nitrogen gas makes up about 78% of the air, but most organisms can’t use it directly. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria, as you saw in the microbes lessons, convert it into forms plants can absorb. Decomposers return nitrogen to the soil, and other bacteria return it to the air.

=== text Keystone species
Some species have a much larger effect on their ecosystem than their numbers suggest. Ecologists call them **keystone species**, after the central stone in an arch that holds the others in place.

In the 1960s, the ecologist **Robert Paine** removed predatory **starfish** from a stretch of rocky shore in Washington State. Without them, mussels took over, crowding out many other species, and diversity fell sharply. He coined the term keystone species.

Another example: **sea otters** on the Pacific coast eat sea urchins. Where otters were hunted out, urchin populations exploded and devoured underwater **kelp forests**, which shelter many fish and other species. Where otters returned, kelp forests recovered.

Predators like **tigers** in India are often considered keystone or “umbrella” species: protecting the large areas of forest they need also protects countless other species, and the rivers and water supplies that people depend on.

=== steps Protecting India’s tigers
- At the start of the 20th century, India may have had tens of thousands of wild tigers. Hunting and habitat loss caused a steep decline.
- In **1973**, India launched **Project Tiger**, creating dedicated tiger reserves, starting with nine.
- Despite setbacks, including serious poaching crises in the 2000s, numbers have recovered. The **2022** national tiger census estimated an average of around **3,600** wild tigers, about three-quarters of the world’s wild tiger population.
- India now has over 50 tiger reserves.
- Challenges remain, including conflict between tigers and people living near forests, habitat fragmentation and ensuring that conservation benefits local and tribal communities rather than displacing them.

=== reveal What are ecosystem services, and why do economists try to put a price on nature?
**Ecosystem services** are the benefits humans get from nature: **provisioning** services like food, fresh water, timber and medicines; **regulating** services like pollination of crops, flood control by wetlands and forests, climate regulation and water purification; and **cultural** services like recreation and spiritual value. Many crops, including many fruits, vegetables and nuts, depend partly on animal pollinators such as bees. Because these services are usually free, markets tend to ignore them, a form of the market failure you met in the Economics region. Putting estimated economic values on them can help decision-makers take nature into account, although critics argue some things, like the existence of a species, can’t or shouldn’t be measured in money.

=== keypoints
- An **ecosystem** is a community of organisms interacting with each other and their environment.
- **Producers** make food from sunlight; **consumers** eat other organisms; **decomposers** recycle dead material.
- **Food webs** connect many food chains; each step is a **trophic level**.
- Only about **10%** of energy passes to the next level, so top predators are few: the **energy pyramid**.
- **Energy flows** through ecosystems, while **nutrients** like carbon and nitrogen **cycle**.
- **Keystone species**, like **Paine’s starfish**, **sea otters** and **wolves**, have outsized effects.
- India’s **Project Tiger (1973)** helped tiger numbers recover to about **3,600** (2022).

=== why
Ecosystems provide the food, water, clean air and stable climate humans depend on. Understanding how energy and nutrients move through them, and how removing one species can cause ripple effects, helps you appreciate why conservation matters, not just for wildlife but for people. The final lesson in this region looks at biodiversity loss and what can be done about it.

##### LESSON
id: biology.ecosystems.2
title: Biodiversity in Crisis, and the Fight to Save It
minutes: 15
prereq: biology.ecosystems.1
check: In your own words: what are the main drivers of biodiversity loss, what does the Living Planet Index measure, and what are two examples of successful conservation?

=== hook
In the early **1970s**, in the hills of what is now Uttarakhand, villagers learned that their local forests had been allocated to commercial loggers. In **1973**, in the village of Mandal, and later in other villages like Reni, people, many of them women led by figures such as **Gaura Devi**, protected the trees by putting themselves between the trees and the loggers, hugging the trunks.

The **Chipko movement**, from the Hindi word for “to stick” or “to cling”, spread across the region. It drew national attention to deforestation, helped lead to restrictions on tree felling in the Himalayan forests, and became an inspiration for environmental movements worldwide. It showed that protecting nature is often about protecting people’s livelihoods too.

=== core
**Biodiversity**, short for biological diversity, is the variety of life on Earth at every level: the diversity of **genes** within species, the diversity of **species**, and the diversity of **ecosystems**.

Scientists have described around **2 million** species, but estimates of the total number of species on Earth, most of them insects, fungi and microbes not yet described, range widely, often from around **8 million** to far more.

Biodiversity matters for many reasons: it underpins the ecosystem services you met in the previous lesson, makes ecosystems more resilient to shocks, provides sources of food and medicine, and, for many people, has deep cultural, spiritual and ethical value in its own right.

=== text The scale of the loss
In **2019**, the **Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES)**, an international scientific body sometimes called the IPCC for biodiversity, published a major global assessment. It estimated that around **1 million** animal and plant species are threatened with extinction, many within decades, unless action is taken.

The **Living Planet Index**, published by WWF and the Zoological Society of London, tracks thousands of populations of vertebrate animals. Its **2024** report found an average decline of about **73%** in the size of monitored wildlife populations since **1970**. This doesn’t mean 73% of animals or species have disappeared; it means that, on average, the tracked populations have shrunk by that much, with some growing and many shrinking sharply. Freshwater species showed especially steep declines.

=== text Five main drivers
The IPBES assessment identified five main direct drivers of biodiversity loss, in roughly this order of global impact:

1. **Changes in land and sea use**: clearing forests, grasslands and wetlands for farming, cities, mining and infrastructure. This is the biggest driver on land.
2. **Direct exploitation**: overfishing, hunting, poaching and logging. This is the biggest driver in the oceans.
3. **Climate change**: shifting temperatures and rainfall patterns, ocean warming and acidification; coral reefs are particularly vulnerable.
4. **Pollution**: plastics, pesticides, fertiliser run-off and other chemicals.
5. **Invasive species**: plants, animals and diseases moved by humans into new places, where they outcompete or prey on native species.

Behind these direct drivers lie deeper causes: growing populations and consumption, economic systems that don’t value nature, and weak governance.

=== callout analogy
Think of an ecosystem as an aeroplane held together by thousands of rivets, each a species. Removing a few rivets might make no visible difference. But keep removing them, and at some point, often unpredictably, a crucial one goes and the plane fails. This analogy, proposed by the ecologists Paul and Anne Ehrlich, captures why scientists worry about losing species even when the immediate effects aren’t obvious.

=== text A sixth mass extinction?
Over Earth’s history, there have been **five mass extinctions**, when a large share of species disappeared in a geologically short time. The most famous, about **66 million years ago**, wiped out the non-bird dinosaurs, most likely after an asteroid impact.

Many scientists warn that current extinction rates are far above natural background rates, and that we may be entering a **sixth mass extinction**, driven by humans. Others argue that, measured by the share of species already lost, we aren’t there yet, but that current trends could lead there. Either way, the rate of loss is widely seen as alarming. The **dodo**, a flightless bird on Mauritius, became extinct by the late 1600s, within a century of humans arriving, and became a symbol of human-caused extinction.

=== steps Conservation successes
- **Asiatic lions**: by the early 20th century, perhaps only a few dozen remained, in India’s **Gir** forest in Gujarat. Protection helped numbers recover to around **670** by **2020**, and further since, though having nearly all of them in one region leaves them vulnerable to disease or disaster.
- **Tigers**: as you saw in the previous lesson, India’s tiger population has grown substantially since Project Tiger began.
- **Whales**: after the international moratorium on commercial whaling in **1986**, some species, like humpback whales, have recovered strongly in many regions.
- **The ozone layer**: although not a species, the **Montreal Protocol** of **1987**, which phased out ozone-destroying chemicals, is widely considered the most successful international environmental agreement, and the ozone layer is slowly healing, protecting all life from harmful UV radiation.
- **Protected areas and community conservation**: many successes come from involving local and Indigenous communities, who manage a large share of the world’s remaining biodiversity, as Elinor Ostrom’s work in the Economics region suggested.

=== text Global goals and hard choices
In **December 2022**, nearly 200 countries adopted the **Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework**, including a target known as **“30 by 30”**: protecting **30%** of the world’s land and oceans by **2030**, along with goals to restore degraded ecosystems, reduce pollution and cut harmful subsidies.

Conservation also involves difficult trade-offs and debates:

- How to protect wildlife without displacing **local and tribal communities** whose livelihoods depend on forests. India’s **Forest Rights Act** of 2006 recognised the rights of forest-dwelling communities, and its implementation is still debated.
- Whether bold interventions work. India’s **Project Cheetah**, which brought cheetahs from Namibia and South Africa to **Kuno National Park** from **2022**, aims to restore a species that went extinct in India in the mid-20th century. Supporters see it as ambitious restoration; critics questioned the habitat and noted that several cheetahs died. Its long-term success is still uncertain.
- How to balance economic development and conservation, especially in lower-income countries.

=== reveal What can individuals actually do about biodiversity loss?
Individual actions are small compared with policy and economic change, but they add up and influence wider change. Options include reducing **food waste**, since farming is the biggest driver of habitat loss; choosing **sustainably produced** food, timber and seafood where possible; eating less meat, especially from sources linked to deforestation, since livestock farming uses a large share of agricultural land; avoiding products from illegally traded wildlife; planting **native** species and reducing pesticide use in gardens to support pollinators; supporting conservation organisations; and, perhaps most powerfully, engaging as a citizen and voter on environmental policies. Learning about local ecosystems and sharing that knowledge also matters: people protect what they understand and care about.

=== keypoints
- **Biodiversity** is the variety of life at the level of **genes**, **species** and **ecosystems**.
- **IPBES (2019)** estimated around **1 million** species threatened with extinction.
- The **Living Planet Index (2024)** found an average **73%** decline in monitored vertebrate populations since **1970**.
- Main drivers: **land and sea use change**, **direct exploitation**, **climate change**, **pollution** and **invasive species**.
- Scientists debate whether a **sixth mass extinction** is under way; current extinction rates are far above natural levels.
- Successes include **Asiatic lions**, **tigers**, **whales** and the **Montreal Protocol**; community-based conservation matters.
- The **Kunming-Montreal framework (2022)** set the **30 by 30** target; conservation involves trade-offs, as with **Project Cheetah** and forest rights.

=== why
Biodiversity loss is one of the defining challenges of this century, closely linked to climate change, food security and human wellbeing. Understanding its causes and the evidence for what works helps you engage with conservation debates thoughtfully, recognise both the scale of the problem and the real possibility of recovery, and see your own choices in a wider context.
