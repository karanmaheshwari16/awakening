##### LESSON
id: physics.atoms.2
title: The Power in the Nucleus
minutes: 15
prereq: physics.atoms.1
check: In your own words: how do fission and fusion release energy, what is a half-life, and what are the main arguments for and against nuclear power?

=== hook
In **December 1938**, in Berlin, the chemists **Otto Hahn** and **Fritz Strassmann** bombarded uranium with neutrons and found something baffling: traces of barium, an element roughly half the size of uranium. They couldn’t explain it.

Hahn wrote to his longtime colleague **Lise Meitner**, an Austrian physicist of Jewish heritage who had recently fled Nazi Germany to Sweden. Walking in the snow with her nephew, the physicist **Otto Frisch**, over the Christmas holidays, Meitner worked out the answer: the uranium nucleus had **split in two**, releasing an enormous amount of energy, calculated using Einstein’s famous equation. They called it **fission**. Within seven years, that discovery would power the first nuclear reactor and destroy two cities. Hahn alone received the Nobel Prize for it, an omission widely regarded today as a serious injustice to Meitner.

=== core
In the previous lesson, you saw that the atomic nucleus is held together by the incredibly powerful **strong nuclear force**. Changing a nucleus, by splitting it, fusing nuclei together or letting it decay, can release vast amounts of energy, far more than chemical reactions like burning fuel.

The key is **Einstein’s equation, E = mc²**, published in **1905**. It says mass and energy are equivalent: a small amount of mass (m) corresponds to an enormous amount of energy (E), because it’s multiplied by the speed of light squared (c²), an immense number.

When nuclei split or fuse in certain ways, the products have slightly **less mass** than the starting materials. The missing mass, called the **mass defect**, is released as energy. Converting just one gram of mass entirely into energy would release about as much as the explosion of roughly **20,000 tonnes** of TNT.

=== text Radioactivity
Some nuclei are unstable and spontaneously change, releasing radiation. This is **radioactivity**, discovered by the French physicist **Henri Becquerel** in **1896**, when he found that uranium salts darkened photographic plates even in the dark.

**Marie Curie**, a Polish-French scientist, and her husband **Pierre Curie** went on to discover two new radioactive elements, **polonium** (named after Poland) and **radium**. Marie Curie became the first person to win **two** Nobel Prizes, in Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911), and remains the only person to win in two different sciences.

There are three main types of radiation:

- **Alpha particles**: two protons and two neutrons. Heavy and slow; stopped by a sheet of paper or the outer layer of skin, but dangerous if swallowed or inhaled.
- **Beta particles**: fast electrons (or their positive counterparts). Stopped by a thin sheet of aluminium.
- **Gamma rays**: high-energy light. Very penetrating; reduced by thick lead or concrete.

=== steps Half-life and carbon dating
- Radioactive decay is random for any single atom, but predictable for large numbers of them.
- The **half-life** is the time it takes for **half** of a radioactive sample to decay.
- **Carbon-14**, a radioactive form of carbon, has a half-life of about **5,730 years**. Living things constantly take in carbon, including a small, steady proportion of carbon-14.
- When an organism dies, it stops taking in carbon, and its carbon-14 slowly decays.
- After 5,730 years, half remains; after about 11,460 years, a quarter; and so on.
- By measuring how much carbon-14 is left in bones, wood or cloth, scientists can estimate how long ago the organism died, up to about **50,000 years**. This technique, developed by **Willard Libby** in the late 1940s, transformed archaeology, and helped date many of the events in the History region.

=== text Fission and the chain reaction
In **fission**, a heavy nucleus, like uranium-235, absorbs a neutron, becomes unstable and splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing energy and **two or three new neutrons**.

Those neutrons can strike other uranium nuclei, causing more fission, releasing more neutrons, and so on: a **chain reaction**. If uncontrolled, it releases energy in a fraction of a second: a nuclear explosion. If carefully controlled, using materials that absorb excess neutrons, it releases energy steadily: a **nuclear reactor**.

The first controlled chain reaction was achieved in **December 1942** by a team led by the Italian-American physicist **Enrico Fermi**, in a pile of uranium and graphite under a sports stadium at the University of Chicago, as part of the wartime Manhattan Project.

=== callout analogy
Think of a chain reaction like a crowded hall full of mousetraps, each loaded with two ping-pong balls. Drop one ball in, and it sets off a trap, which launches two balls, which set off two more traps, and so on, until the whole hall erupts in seconds. A nuclear reactor is like adding nets that catch most of the flying balls, so that each trap sets off, on average, exactly one more, keeping the reaction steady.

=== text Nuclear power
A nuclear power station uses the heat from controlled fission to boil water, producing steam that drives turbines to generate electricity, much like a coal plant, but without burning fuel or emitting carbon dioxide during operation.

Nuclear power provides around **9%** of the world’s electricity. **France** gets roughly two-thirds of its electricity from nuclear power. **India** has a long-term nuclear programme, envisioned by the physicist **Homi Bhabha** in the 1950s as a **three-stage plan** designed eventually to use India’s large reserves of **thorium**, since the country has limited uranium.

The debate over nuclear power is intense:

- **For**: very low carbon emissions; reliable power regardless of weather; a small land footprint; and, measured by deaths per unit of electricity produced, one of the **safest** energy sources, comparable to wind and solar and far safer than coal, oil and gas, which kill many people through air pollution.
- **Against**: rare but serious accidents, like **Chernobyl** in the Soviet Union (1986) and **Fukushima** in Japan (2011, triggered by an earthquake and tsunami), which forced large evacuations; long-lived **radioactive waste** that must be stored safely for thousands of years; high construction costs and long building times; and links to nuclear weapons proliferation.

=== text Fusion: powering the stars
**Fusion** is the opposite of fission: light nuclei, such as forms of hydrogen, join together to form heavier ones, releasing energy. Fusion powers the **Sun** and all stars, as you saw in the Space region.

Achieving fusion on Earth requires temperatures of over **100 million degrees Celsius**, hotter than the Sun’s core, to force nuclei close enough together. The fuel is abundant, fusion produces no long-lived high-level waste, and a runaway chain reaction isn’t possible.

In **December 2022**, the **National Ignition Facility** in California achieved a milestone: for the first time, a fusion experiment released more energy (about **3.15 megajoules**) than the laser energy delivered to the fuel (about **2.05 megajoules**). However, the lasers themselves used far more energy from the grid, so practical fusion power is still likely decades away. The international **ITER** project in France, with India among its members, is building a large fusion reactor to test the technology at scale.

=== reveal Are you exposed to radiation every day?
Yes. Everyone is constantly exposed to low levels of natural **background radiation**: from radon gas seeping out of rocks and soil, cosmic rays from space, radioactive elements in the ground and building materials, and even from your own body, which contains small amounts of radioactive potassium and carbon. A banana contains a tiny amount of radioactive potassium-40. The average person worldwide receives a few millisieverts of natural radiation a year, varying greatly by location; parts of Kerala, India, have unusually high natural background levels because of thorium-rich sands. A chest X-ray adds a small dose; a CT scan considerably more. Low doses carry very small risks; high doses can cause radiation sickness and increase cancer risk, which is why medical imaging is used when its benefits outweigh those small risks.

=== keypoints
- **E = mc²** (Einstein, 1905): tiny amounts of mass correspond to enormous energy; nuclear reactions release energy from a **mass defect**.
- **Radioactivity** was discovered by **Becquerel (1896)**; **Marie Curie** discovered polonium and radium and won two Nobel Prizes.
- **Alpha**, **beta** and **gamma** radiation differ in penetrating power.
- **Half-life** enables **carbon-14 dating** (half-life ~5,730 years).
- **Fission** splits heavy nuclei; a controlled **chain reaction** powers reactors (Fermi, **1942**).
- **Nuclear power** is low-carbon and statistically very safe but raises concerns about accidents, waste and cost.
- **Fusion** powers stars; the **NIF** achieved a net energy gain from the fuel in **2022**, but practical fusion power remains distant.

=== why
Nuclear physics shapes major decisions about energy, climate and security. Understanding how fission, fusion and radiation actually work helps you evaluate debates about nuclear power, nuclear weapons and medical imaging, beyond fear or hype. And it reveals the extraordinary energy locked inside every atom, the same energy that makes the stars shine.

##### LESSON
id: physics.light.1
title: Light and the Electromagnetic Spectrum
minutes: 14
check: In your own words: what are wavelength and frequency, what is the electromagnetic spectrum, and why is the sky blue?

=== hook
In the **1660s**, a young **Isaac Newton**, staying at home in the countryside while Cambridge University was closed by the plague, darkened his room, let a thin beam of sunlight in through a hole in the shutter, and passed it through a triangular glass prism. On the opposite wall appeared a band of colours: red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet.

Others had seen prisms create colours before, but most thought the glass was adding colour to white light. Newton showed the opposite: white light is a **mixture** of all colours, and the prism simply separates them. He then used a second prism to recombine the colours back into white light. It was a simple experiment that revealed something fundamental about light.

=== core
Light is a form of energy that travels as a **wave**, specifically an **electromagnetic wave**, made of oscillating electric and magnetic fields. Unlike sound or water waves, it doesn’t need a medium to travel through; it can cross the vacuum of space, which is how sunlight reaches us.

Every wave can be described by a few properties:

- **Wavelength**: the distance between one wave peak and the next.
- **Frequency**: how many wave peaks pass a point each second, measured in **hertz (Hz)**.
- **Amplitude**: the height of the wave, related to brightness for light.

For light, wavelength and frequency are linked: the shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency. And all light travels at the same speed in a vacuum: about **299,792 kilometres per second**, the **speed of light**, the fastest speed possible in the universe.

=== text How fast is light?
For most of history, people assumed light travelled instantly. In **1676**, the Danish astronomer **Ole Rømer** noticed that eclipses of Jupiter’s moon Io came slightly earlier when Earth was closer to Jupiter and later when it was farther away. He realised the difference was the extra time light took to cross the greater distance, and made one of the first estimates of the speed of light.

At the speed of light, you could circle the Earth about **seven and a half times in one second**. Light from the Moon takes just over a second to reach us; from the Sun, about eight minutes.

=== text The electromagnetic spectrum
The light our eyes can see is just a tiny slice of a vast range of electromagnetic waves, called the **electromagnetic spectrum**. All of them travel at the speed of light; they differ only in wavelength and frequency:

- **Radio waves**: the longest wavelengths, from millimetres to kilometres. Used for radio, television and mobile communication.
- **Microwaves**: used in microwave ovens, Wi-Fi, mobile networks and radar.
- **Infrared**: felt as heat. Used in TV remote controls and thermal cameras.
- **Visible light**: wavelengths from about **400 nanometres** (violet) to **700 nanometres** (red).
- **Ultraviolet (UV)**: causes sunburn and helps the skin make vitamin D.
- **X-rays**: pass through soft tissue but are blocked by bones, so they’re used for medical imaging.
- **Gamma rays**: the shortest wavelengths and highest energy, produced by radioactive decay and violent cosmic events.

=== callout analogy
Think of the electromagnetic spectrum as a gigantic piano keyboard, stretching for kilometres. Radio waves are the low notes at one end, gamma rays the highest notes at the other. Visible light is a single octave somewhere in the middle, the only part our eyes can “hear”. Other animals hear a bit more: bees see ultraviolet, and some snakes sense infrared.

=== text Bending and bouncing light
**Reflection**: light bounces off surfaces. A smooth surface, like a mirror, reflects it neatly so you see an image. The angle at which light hits the surface equals the angle at which it bounces off.

**Refraction**: when light passes from one material into another, like from air into water or glass, it changes speed and bends. That’s why a straw looks bent in a glass of water, and how lenses in glasses, cameras and microscopes focus light.

One of the great pioneers of optics was **Ibn al-Haytham** (known in Latin as Alhazen), a scholar working in Cairo in the early 11th century. His **Book of Optics** argued, from careful experiments, that we see because light travels **into** our eyes from objects, rather than our eyes sending out rays, as many earlier thinkers believed. He studied reflection, refraction and the **camera obscura**, and his emphasis on experiment has led some to call him an early pioneer of the scientific method.

=== steps How a rainbow forms
- Sunlight, a mixture of all colours, enters a raindrop.
- As it enters, it **refracts** (bends), and each colour bends by a slightly different amount: violet bends most, red least.
- The light **reflects** off the back of the raindrop.
- It refracts again as it leaves, spreading the colours further apart.
- Each raindrop sends light of different colours out at slightly different angles. You see red from drops higher in the sky and violet from drops lower down, forming an arc.
- You only see a rainbow with the Sun **behind** you and rain in front of you. Every person sees their own rainbow, from a slightly different set of raindrops.

=== reveal Why is the sky blue, and why are sunsets red?
Sunlight passing through the atmosphere hits gas molecules, mainly nitrogen and oxygen, which are much smaller than the wavelength of light. They scatter **shorter** wavelengths, like blue, much more strongly than longer ones, like red. This is called **Rayleigh scattering**, after the British physicist Lord Rayleigh. Blue light gets scattered all across the sky, so wherever you look, you see blue. (Violet is scattered even more, but sunlight contains less violet and our eyes are less sensitive to it.) At sunset, sunlight travels through much more atmosphere to reach you, so most of the blue is scattered away before it arrives, leaving the reds and oranges. In **1928**, the Indian physicist **C. V. Raman** discovered a different kind of light scattering, in which a tiny fraction of light changes wavelength, now called the **Raman effect**. He won the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physics, the first Asian scientist to win a Nobel in science. India celebrates National Science Day on 28 February to mark his discovery.

=== keypoints
- Light is an **electromagnetic wave** that can travel through a vacuum at about **299,792 km/s**.
- Waves have **wavelength**, **frequency** and **amplitude**; shorter wavelength means higher frequency.
- **Newton** showed white light is a mixture of colours; **Rømer (1676)** made an early estimate of light’s speed.
- The **electromagnetic spectrum** runs from **radio waves** to **gamma rays**; visible light is about **400–700 nm**.
- **Reflection** bounces light; **refraction** bends it; **Ibn al-Haytham** pioneered experimental optics.
- **Rainbows** come from refraction and reflection in raindrops; the sky is blue due to **Rayleigh scattering**.
- **C. V. Raman** discovered the **Raman effect** (Nobel Prize, 1930).

=== why
Light is how we see the world and how most information about the universe reaches us, from distant galaxies to medical images of our own bodies. The electromagnetic spectrum underpins radio, mobile phones, Wi-Fi, microwaves, X-rays and much more. Next, you’ll discover that light is even stranger than it seems: it sometimes behaves like a particle.

##### LESSON
id: physics.light.2
title: Wave or Particle? The Strange Nature of Light
minutes: 15
prereq: physics.light.1
check: In your own words: what did Young’s double-slit experiment show, how did the photoelectric effect suggest light is made of particles, and what does wave-particle duality mean?

=== hook
In **1905**, a 26-year-old patent clerk in Bern, Switzerland, published several papers that would transform physics. One introduced special relativity. Another explained the jiggling motion of tiny particles in water, helping prove atoms exist. A third, which Einstein himself called “very revolutionary”, proposed that light, long proven to be a wave, also comes in tiny packets of energy.

Many physicists found the idea absurd. Yet experiments eventually confirmed it, and in **1921**, Albert Einstein was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics, not for relativity, as many assume, but for this explanation of the **photoelectric effect**. It opened the door to quantum physics, and to the bizarre idea that light is somehow both a wave and a particle.

=== core
For centuries, scientists argued about the nature of light. **Isaac Newton** believed light was made of tiny particles, which he called corpuscles. The Dutch scientist **Christiaan Huygens** argued light was a wave. Newton’s enormous reputation meant the particle view dominated for about a century.

Then the evidence swung decisively towards waves, before quantum physics revealed that the truth is stranger than either side imagined.

=== text Young’s double slit: light as a wave
In **1801**, the English polymath **Thomas Young** shone light through two narrow, closely spaced slits onto a screen. If light were made of simple particles, you might expect two bright lines, one behind each slit.

Instead, he saw a pattern of many alternating **bright and dark bands**. This is exactly what waves do. Where the peaks of waves from the two slits meet, they combine to make a brighter wave (**constructive interference**). Where a peak meets a trough, they cancel out, making darkness (**destructive interference**). You can see the same effect when ripples from two stones dropped in a pond overlap.

Young’s experiment was powerful evidence that light is a wave.

=== callout analogy
Picture two people dropping pebbles into a still pond at the same rhythm, a short distance apart. Two sets of circular ripples spread out and overlap. In some places, the ripples add up into big waves; in others, they cancel to calm water. Along the far bank, you’d see a pattern of rough and calm patches. Young’s bright and dark bands are that pattern, made of light.

=== text Maxwell’s triumph
In the **1860s**, the Scottish physicist **James Clerk Maxwell** developed a set of equations describing electricity and magnetism. They predicted that changing electric and magnetic fields could create waves that travel through space, and calculated their speed. It came out almost exactly equal to the measured speed of light.

Maxwell concluded that **light is an electromagnetic wave**. In **1887**, the German physicist **Heinrich Hertz** produced and detected radio waves in his laboratory, confirming Maxwell’s prediction. The wave theory seemed complete.

=== text The photoelectric effect: light as particles
But one experiment didn’t fit. When light shines on certain metals, it can knock electrons out of the surface. This is the **photoelectric effect**. Experiments showed puzzling results:

- **Dim blue or ultraviolet light** could knock electrons out immediately.
- **Bright red light**, no matter how intense, couldn’t knock out any electrons at all.

If light were simply a wave, brighter light should carry more energy and eventually knock electrons out, whatever its colour. Einstein proposed a radical explanation, building on earlier work by Max Planck: light comes in tiny packets of energy, later called **photons**. Each photon’s energy depends on the light’s **frequency**: higher-frequency light, like blue and ultraviolet, has more energetic photons.

An electron can only be knocked out if a **single photon** has enough energy. Red photons individually don’t have enough, so no matter how many of them arrive, no electrons escape. A single ultraviolet photon does.

=== steps Why photon energy matters in daily life
- A photon’s energy is proportional to its frequency: higher frequency, more energy per photon.
- **Radio waves and microwaves** have very low-energy photons, too weak to break chemical bonds in your cells. At high power, microwaves can heat things, but they don’t damage DNA directly the way ionising radiation can.
- **Visible light** photons have enough energy to trigger the light-sensitive molecules in your eyes, and to drive photosynthesis in plants.
- **Ultraviolet** photons carry enough energy to damage DNA in skin cells, which is why UV causes sunburn and raises skin cancer risk, and why sunscreen matters.
- **X-rays and gamma rays** have very high-energy photons that can knock electrons out of atoms, called **ionising radiation**, which is why exposure is carefully limited.
- It’s not just how bright the light is, but the energy of each photon, that determines what it can do.

=== text Wave-particle duality
So is light a wave or a particle? The answer from quantum physics is: **both, and neither**. Light shows wave-like behaviour, like interference, in some experiments, and particle-like behaviour, like knocking out single electrons, in others. This is called **wave-particle duality**.

The strangeness deepens when you send light through Young’s double slits **one photon at a time**. Each photon hits the screen at a single point, like a particle. But as thousands of photons arrive, one by one, they gradually build up the same pattern of bright and dark bands, as though each single photon somehow passed through **both** slits and interfered with itself. Even more strikingly, the same happens with **electrons**, and even with whole molecules: matter, too, has wave-like properties. You’ll explore what this means in the quantum lessons.

=== text Light at work: lasers and fibre optics
Understanding light as photons led to remarkable technologies.

**Lasers**, first built by **Theodore Maiman** in **1960**, produce intense, focused beams of light in which photons are all of the same wavelength and move in step. They’re used in barcode scanners, surgery, eye correction, cutting metal, measuring distances and reading data.

**Fibre-optic cables** carry information as pulses of light through thin strands of glass, bouncing along inside by a process called total internal reflection. The Indian-born physicist **Narinder Singh Kapany** did pioneering work on fibre optics in the 1950s, and **Charles Kao** won the 2009 Nobel Prize for showing how glass fibres could carry signals over long distances. Today, fibre-optic cables carry most of the world’s internet traffic, including the undersea cables you met in the Technology region.

=== reveal Why do solar panels work in sunlight but not under a bright red lamp of the same brightness?
Solar panels work on a principle closely related to the photoelectric effect, called the **photovoltaic effect**. Photons hitting a specially made semiconductor material, usually silicon, give electrons enough energy to move and create an electric current. As with the photoelectric effect, each photon needs a minimum energy. Sunlight contains many photons across the spectrum with enough energy, while a lamp emitting only lower-energy red light would produce much less current, even if it looked equally bright. This is also why panels are designed to absorb as wide a range of photon energies as possible, and why solar energy technology depends directly on Einstein’s 1905 insight.

=== keypoints
- **Newton** thought light was particles; **Huygens** thought it was waves.
- **Young’s double-slit experiment (1801)** showed **interference**, strong evidence for waves.
- **Maxwell (1860s)** showed light is an **electromagnetic wave**; **Hertz (1887)** confirmed radio waves.
- The **photoelectric effect** showed light comes in packets, **photons**, whose energy depends on **frequency** (**Einstein, 1905**; Nobel Prize 1921).
- Light shows **wave-particle duality**; single photons and even electrons build up interference patterns.
- Photon energy explains why **UV** damages skin while radio waves don’t.
- **Lasers** (1960) and **fibre optics** (Kapany, Kao) depend on understanding light.

=== why
The question “what is light?” led directly to quantum physics, one of the most successful and strangest theories in science. It also underpins technologies you use daily, including solar panels, lasers, cameras and fibre-optic internet. Understanding photon energy helps you make sense of real risks, like UV exposure, and false fears, like claims that ordinary radio waves damage DNA.

##### LESSON
id: physics.electricity.1
title: Electricity: Charges, Currents and Circuits
minutes: 14
check: In your own words: what are current, voltage and resistance, how are they related by Ohm’s law, and how would you calculate the cost of running an appliance?

=== hook
Around **600 BCE**, the Greek philosopher **Thales of Miletus** reportedly noticed that rubbing amber with fur made it attract light objects like feathers and straw. The Greek word for amber was *elektron*, the origin of our words “electron” and “electricity”.

For over two thousand years, this static attraction remained a curiosity. Then, in **1800**, the Italian scientist **Alessandro Volta** stacked discs of zinc and copper separated by cloth soaked in salt water and produced the first steady electric current: the first battery. The unit of electrical potential, the **volt**, is named after him. Within a century, electricity would light cities, power factories and carry messages across oceans.

=== core
**Electricity** is the presence and flow of **electric charge**. Charge comes in two types: **positive** and **negative**. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract.

As you saw in the atoms lessons, atoms contain positive protons in the nucleus and negative electrons around it. Usually, they balance. But electrons, especially the outer ones in some materials, can be moved:

- **Static electricity** happens when charge builds up on a surface, like when you rub a balloon on your hair or get a small shock touching a metal doorknob. Lightning is static electricity on an enormous scale.
- **Current electricity** happens when charge flows continuously, usually electrons moving through a wire. This is what powers your devices.

**Conductors**, like copper and aluminium, let electrons move easily. **Insulators**, like rubber, plastic and glass, don’t. Wires are made of copper covered in plastic for this reason.

=== text Three key quantities
**Current (I)**: the rate at which charge flows, measured in **amperes (amps, A)**. It’s like the amount of water flowing through a pipe each second.

**Voltage (V)**: the “push” that drives current through a circuit, measured in **volts (V)**. More precisely, it’s the energy given to each unit of charge. It’s like the water pressure in a pipe. Household supplies are about **230 volts** in India, the UK and much of the world, and about 120 volts in the US.

**Resistance (R)**: how much a material opposes the flow of current, measured in **ohms (Ω)**. It’s like a narrow section of pipe restricting the flow. Thin, long wires have more resistance than thick, short ones. Resistance turns electrical energy into heat, which is useful in kettles and heaters, and wasteful elsewhere.

=== callout analogy
Think of an electric circuit as a water system. The battery or power supply is a **pump** creating pressure (**voltage**). The water flowing through the pipes is the **current**. Narrow sections of pipe, like a tap partly closed, are **resistance**. More pressure pushes more water through; more restriction lets less through. The analogy isn’t perfect, but it captures the key relationships.

=== text Ohm’s law
In **1827**, the German physicist **Georg Ohm** found a simple relationship between these quantities for many materials:

**Voltage = Current × Resistance**, or **V = I × R**.

This means:

- Increase the voltage, and more current flows (for the same resistance).
- Increase the resistance, and less current flows (for the same voltage).

For example, a 230-volt supply connected to a heating element with a resistance of 23 ohms drives a current of 230 ÷ 23 = **10 amps**.

=== text Circuits: series and parallel
A **circuit** is a complete loop that current can flow around. If the loop is broken, as when you switch something off, current stops.

- **Series circuits**: components are connected one after another in a single loop. The same current flows through each. If one component breaks, the whole circuit stops. Old-fashioned strings of festival lights sometimes worked this way: one dead bulb made them all go out.
- **Parallel circuits**: components are connected on separate branches. Each gets the full voltage, and if one fails, the others keep working. Houses are wired in parallel, so turning off one light doesn’t turn off the others.

=== steps Worked example: what does it cost to run a heater?
- Electrical **power** (in watts) = **voltage × current**, or P = V × I.
- A heater drawing **10 amps** from a **230-volt** supply uses 230 × 10 = **2,300 watts**, or **2.3 kilowatts**.
- Electricity is billed in **kilowatt-hours (kWh)**: power in kilowatts multiplied by hours of use.
- Running the heater for **3 hours** uses 2.3 × 3 = **6.9 kWh**.
- If electricity costs **8** (in your local currency) per kWh, that’s 6.9 × 8 ≈ **55** for the evening.
- Compare a **10-watt** LED bulb left on for those same 3 hours: 0.01 × 3 = 0.03 kWh, costing about **0.24**. Devices that **heat** or **cool**, such as heaters, geysers, air conditioners, irons and ovens, usually dominate electricity bills.

=== text Electricity and safety
Electricity is dangerous because current passing through the body can disrupt the heart’s rhythm and cause burns. It’s the **current** through the body that does the damage, and surprisingly small currents, tens of thousandths of an amp, flowing across the chest can be fatal. Dry skin has fairly high resistance, which offers some protection; wet skin has much lower resistance, which is why electricity near water is so dangerous.

Homes use several safety features:

- **Fuses and circuit breakers** cut off the current if too much flows, preventing overheating and fires.
- **Earthing** (grounding) provides a safe path for current to flow into the ground if a fault occurs, rather than through a person.
- **Residual current devices (RCDs)**, also called earth-leakage circuit breakers, detect current leaking where it shouldn’t and cut the power within a fraction of a second.

=== reveal Birds sit on high-voltage power lines without being harmed. Why?
Because current needs a **difference in voltage** to flow, and a complete path. A bird perched on a single wire has both feet at essentially the same voltage, so almost no current flows through its body; the current simply continues along the wire, which offers far less resistance. The danger comes if the bird, or a person, touches the wire and something at a different voltage at the same time, like another wire, a pole or the ground. Then current can flow through the body. That’s why large birds with wide wingspans are sometimes electrocuted when they touch two wires at once, and why people should stay far away from fallen power lines.

=== keypoints
- **Electricity** is the presence and flow of **charge**; like charges repel, opposite charges attract.
- **Static electricity** is built-up charge; **current electricity** is flowing charge. **Volta** built the first battery in **1800**.
- **Current** (amps) is flow; **voltage** (volts) is push; **resistance** (ohms) opposes flow.
- **Ohm’s law**: **V = I × R** (Ohm, 1827).
- **Series** circuits share one loop; **parallel** circuits have separate branches, as in homes.
- **Power = V × I** in watts; energy bills use **kilowatt-hours**; heating and cooling dominate costs.
- **Fuses**, **earthing** and **RCDs** protect against dangerous currents.

=== why
Electricity powers almost every part of modern life. Understanding current, voltage and resistance helps you use appliances safely, understand your electricity bill and make sense of energy debates. Next, you’ll discover the deep connection between electricity and magnetism, and how power stations and the grid deliver electricity to your home.
