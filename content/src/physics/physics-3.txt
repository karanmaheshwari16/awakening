##### LESSON
id: physics.electricity.2
title: Magnetism, Generators and the Grid
minutes: 15
prereq: physics.electricity.1
check: In your own words: how does a generator turn motion into electricity, why is electricity transmitted at very high voltages, and why must the grid constantly balance supply and demand?

=== hook
In **1820**, during a lecture in Copenhagen, the Danish physicist **Hans Christian Ørsted** noticed something odd. When he switched on an electric current in a wire, a nearby compass needle twitched and swung away from north. When he switched the current off, the needle swung back.

It was a small movement with enormous consequences. Electricity and magnetism, long thought to be separate phenomena, were connected. Within about a decade, that connection would be used to build the first electric motors and generators. Today, nearly all the electricity in the world, whether from coal, gas, nuclear, hydro or wind, is produced using the principle that grew from Ørsted’s twitching compass.

=== core
**Magnetism** is a force produced by moving electric charges. Magnets have two poles, **north** and **south**; like poles repel, opposite poles attract. Around every magnet is a **magnetic field**, the region where its force acts, which you can see by sprinkling iron filings around a bar magnet.

Ørsted’s discovery showed that an **electric current creates a magnetic field**. Coil a current-carrying wire around an iron core, and you get an **electromagnet**: a magnet you can switch on and off. Electromagnets are used in scrapyard cranes, MRI scanners, loudspeakers, electric bells and countless motors.

The reverse is also true, and it’s even more important: **a changing magnetic field can create an electric current**.

=== text Faraday and electromagnetic induction
In **1831**, the English scientist **Michael Faraday**, who had little formal education and began his career as a bookbinder’s apprentice, discovered **electromagnetic induction**. When he moved a magnet in and out of a coil of wire, a current flowed in the wire. Hold the magnet still, and the current stopped. It was the **change** in magnetic field that mattered.

This is the principle behind the **generator**: rotate a coil of wire in a magnetic field (or rotate magnets near a coil), and electricity flows. Faraday also built early **motors**, which work the opposite way: current flowing through a coil in a magnetic field makes it turn, converting electrical energy into motion.

Later, **James Clerk Maxwell** combined electricity and magnetism into a single theory of **electromagnetism**, which, as you saw in the light lessons, also explained light itself.

=== callout analogy
Think of electricity and magnetism as dance partners. When one moves, the other responds. A moving current makes a magnetic field swirl around it; a moving magnet drives a current through a wire. A generator spins the dance to produce electricity from motion; a motor reverses it to produce motion from electricity.

=== steps How a power station makes electricity
- Most power stations ultimately do one thing: **spin a turbine**.
- In coal, gas and nuclear plants, heat boils water into high-pressure steam, which pushes the blades of a turbine.
- In hydroelectric plants, falling water spins the turbine; in wind farms, moving air spins the blades.
- The turbine is connected to a **generator**, where powerful magnets rotate near coils of wire (or coils rotate near magnets).
- The changing magnetic field induces an electric current in the coils, by Faraday’s principle.
- **Solar panels** are the main exception: they turn light directly into electricity using the photovoltaic effect you met in the light lessons, with no spinning parts at all.

=== text AC versus DC: the war of the currents
Electricity can flow in two ways:

- **Direct current (DC)** flows steadily in one direction, as from a battery.
- **Alternating current (AC)** rapidly reverses direction, many times a second. Generators naturally produce AC.

In the **1880s and 1890s**, a fierce rivalry, later called the **war of the currents**, broke out in the US. **Thomas Edison** promoted DC systems. **Nikola Tesla**, a Serbian-American inventor who developed key AC technologies, and the businessman **George Westinghouse** championed AC. Edison’s side emphasised the dangers of high-voltage AC.

AC won for most power grids, largely because of the **transformer**, a device that can easily raise or lower AC voltage. That turned out to be crucial for sending electricity over long distances.

=== text Why power lines use very high voltages
When electricity flows through wires, some energy is lost as heat because of resistance. The losses depend on the **current**: in fact, they increase with the **square** of the current. Double the current and you quadruple the losses.

For the same amount of power (power = voltage × current), you can use **high voltage and low current** instead of low voltage and high current. So power stations use transformers to step voltage **up**, often to hundreds of thousands of volts, for long-distance transmission, then substations step it back **down** to safer levels, such as 230 volts, for homes. This is why you see tall pylons carrying high-voltage lines across the countryside.

=== text The grid: a balancing act
A **power grid** is a network connecting power stations to homes and businesses, often across an entire country or several countries.

The grid has a remarkable constraint: electricity must be generated at almost **exactly the same moment** it’s used, because large-scale storage has historically been limited. If demand exceeds supply, the grid’s **frequency**, which is about **50 hertz** (cycles per second) in India, the UK and Europe, and **60 hertz** in the US, starts to drop. If it falls too far, equipment can be damaged and blackouts can spread. Grid operators constantly adjust supply to match demand, minute by minute.

India completed the synchronisation of its regional grids into a single national grid, operating at one frequency, in **2013**, under the vision of “**One Nation, One Grid**”, one of the largest synchronised grids in the world. A year earlier, in **2012**, India experienced one of the largest blackouts in history, affecting hundreds of millions of people, after grid disturbances spread across northern and eastern regions.

=== reveal Solar and wind power vary with the weather. How can a grid cope?
This is one of the central challenges of the energy transition. Grid operators use several approaches: **storage**, especially large **batteries**, whose costs have fallen dramatically, and **pumped hydro**, which pumps water uphill when power is plentiful and releases it through turbines when needed; **flexible generation**, like gas or hydro plants that can ramp up quickly; **wider grids** that connect regions with different weather; **demand response**, encouraging users to shift consumption, like charging electric vehicles, to times when power is plentiful; and better **forecasting** of wind and sunshine. Combining these makes it possible to run grids with high shares of renewable energy, though the best mix depends on each country’s resources.

=== text Earth: a giant magnet
Earth itself has a magnetic field, generated by the movement of molten iron in its outer core. That’s why compass needles point roughly north, a fact used for navigation for around a thousand years, with early compasses developed in China.

Earth’s magnetic field also forms the **magnetosphere**, which deflects much of the stream of charged particles from the Sun, called the **solar wind**. Where some particles funnel in near the poles, they make the atmosphere glow in the beautiful **auroras**, the northern and southern lights. Strong solar storms can disrupt satellites, radio communication and even power grids.

=== keypoints
- **Ørsted (1820)** discovered that electric current creates a **magnetic field**; electromagnets use this.
- **Faraday (1831)** discovered **electromagnetic induction**: a changing magnetic field creates current, the basis of **generators** and **motors**.
- Most power stations **spin turbines** connected to generators; **solar panels** are the main exception.
- **AC** won the **war of the currents** (Edison vs Tesla and Westinghouse) because **transformers** change AC voltage easily.
- High-voltage transmission reduces losses, which rise with the **square of the current**.
- Grids must balance supply and demand continuously; frequency is **50 Hz** in India, the UK and Europe and **60 Hz** in the US. India unified its grid in **2013**.
- Earth’s magnetic field guides compasses and shields us from the **solar wind**.

=== why
Every time you switch on a light, you rely on Faraday’s discovery and on a vast grid balancing supply and demand in real time. Understanding how electricity is generated and delivered helps you make sense of power cuts, energy prices and debates about renewable energy, grids and storage, which are central to tackling climate change.

##### LESSON
id: physics.quantum.1
title: The Quantum World
minutes: 15
check: In your own words: what does it mean that energy is quantised, what is superposition, and what does the uncertainty principle say?

=== hook
In **1924**, a young physicist in Dacca (now Dhaka, in Bangladesh), **Satyendra Nath Bose**, wrote a short paper deriving an important formula in a new way, by treating particles of light as identical in a way nobody had before. Journals rejected it. So he sent it directly to **Albert Einstein**.

Einstein immediately recognised its importance, translated it into German himself and arranged its publication. He then extended Bose’s idea to atoms, predicting that at extremely low temperatures, atoms could merge into a single quantum state. Seventy years later, in **1995**, scientists in the US finally created this **Bose–Einstein condensate**. Today, a whole family of particles is named **bosons** in Bose’s honour. His story is a glimpse into quantum physics: the strange set of rules that govern the very small.

=== core
**Quantum mechanics** is the branch of physics that describes how matter and energy behave at the scale of atoms and smaller particles. It was developed in the early 20th century by physicists including **Max Planck**, **Albert Einstein**, **Niels Bohr**, **Werner Heisenberg**, **Erwin Schrödinger**, **Paul Dirac** and many others.

At everyday scales, the world seems smooth and predictable, following Newton’s laws. At the quantum scale, it behaves in ways that seem to defy common sense. Yet quantum mechanics is one of the most precisely tested theories in all of science, and it underpins technologies from lasers and computer chips to MRI scanners and LEDs.

=== text Idea 1: quantisation
In **1900**, the German physicist **Max Planck** was trying to explain the light given off by hot objects. He found that the maths only worked if energy was emitted in tiny, separate packets, which he called **quanta**, rather than continuously. Planck considered it a mathematical trick at first. Einstein then used the idea to explain the photoelectric effect in 1905, as you saw in the light lessons.

In **1913**, **Niels Bohr** applied quantisation to atoms. He proposed that electrons can only occupy certain specific **energy levels**, like rungs on a ladder, not anywhere in between. When an electron jumps down from a higher level to a lower one, it emits a photon with exactly the energy difference between the levels.

That explains why each element gives off light at its own precise set of colours, its **spectral lines**, like a fingerprint. Astronomers use these fingerprints to work out what distant stars are made of; sodium streetlights glow yellow-orange for the same reason.

=== callout analogy
Think of the difference between a ramp and a staircase. On a ramp, you can stand at any height. On a staircase, you can only stand on the steps, never halfway between. In the quantum world, energy often behaves like the staircase: an electron in an atom can only be on certain steps, and when it jumps between them, it gives off or absorbs exactly the energy of the gap.

=== text Idea 2: superposition and probability
In quantum mechanics, particles are described by a **wave function**, a mathematical object developed by **Erwin Schrödinger** in 1926. The wave function doesn’t tell you exactly where a particle is; it tells you the **probability** of finding it in different places when you measure it.

Before measurement, a quantum system can be in a **superposition**: a combination of several possible states at once. An electron might be in a superposition of being in two places, or spinning in two directions. When it’s measured, you only ever find one definite result, with probabilities given by the wave function.

This is what the double-slit experiment with single particles suggested in the light lessons: each particle seems to explore both paths at once, interfering with itself, until it’s detected at one particular spot.

=== text Schrödinger’s cat
In **1935**, Schrödinger proposed a thought experiment to show how strange this seemed. A cat is sealed in a box with a radioactive atom, a detector and a vial of poison. If the atom decays, which has a 50% chance in an hour, the detector triggers the poison, killing the cat. If quantum superposition applied to everything, then until someone opens the box, the cat would be in a superposition of being **both alive and dead**.

Schrödinger intended this as a criticism, highlighting what he saw as an absurdity in how quantum mechanics was being interpreted. It became one of the most famous images in science. Most physicists today think that interactions with the environment, a process called **decoherence**, rapidly destroy superpositions in large objects like cats, which is why we never see them in everyday life. But exactly how and why measurement produces definite results is still debated, as the next lesson explains.

=== text Idea 3: the uncertainty principle
In **1927**, the German physicist **Werner Heisenberg** formulated the **uncertainty principle**: certain pairs of properties, like a particle’s **position** and **momentum**, cannot both be known precisely at the same time. The more precisely you pin down one, the less precisely you can know the other.

This isn’t just a limit of our measuring instruments. It’s a fundamental feature of nature: particles simply don’t have perfectly defined positions and momenta at the same time. The uncertainty is tiny for everyday objects, so you never notice it, but it’s crucial for atoms. It helps explain why electrons don’t simply spiral into the nucleus: confining an electron too tightly would give it a huge spread of momentum, and therefore energy.

=== steps Quantum tunnelling: walking through walls
- In everyday life, if you roll a ball at a hill without enough energy to get over the top, it rolls back. It can never appear on the other side.
- In the quantum world, particles have a small probability of passing straight through an energy barrier they shouldn’t have enough energy to cross. This is called **quantum tunnelling**.
- Tunnelling helps power the **Sun**. At its core, protons don’t quite have enough energy to overcome their electrical repulsion and fuse. Tunnelling lets some of them do it anyway, which is why stars shine.
- It’s also used in technology: **flash memory** in phones and USB drives relies on electrons tunnelling through thin insulating layers to store data, and **scanning tunnelling microscopes** use it to image individual atoms.
- As computer chips shrink, unwanted tunnelling, with electrons leaking through ultra-thin barriers, becomes one of the challenges engineers face.

=== reveal If quantum mechanics is so strange, how do we know it’s right?
Because its predictions have been tested with extraordinary precision, and it has never failed a well-designed experiment. One measure of an electron’s magnetic properties, calculated using quantum theory, matches experiments to around **one part in a trillion**, among the most accurate predictions in all of science. Quantum mechanics also explains the periodic table and chemistry, why some materials conduct electricity and others don’t, and how lasers, transistors, LEDs and MRI scanners work. A large share of modern technology depends on it. The strangeness is in what it says about reality, not in whether it works.

=== keypoints
- **Quantum mechanics** describes the behaviour of matter and energy at the scale of atoms and smaller.
- **Planck (1900)**: energy comes in **quanta**. **Bohr (1913)**: electrons occupy specific **energy levels**, explaining **spectral lines**.
- **Satyendra Nath Bose** (1924) pioneered new quantum statistics; **bosons** are named after him.
- The **wave function** (Schrödinger, 1926) gives **probabilities**; systems can be in **superposition** until measured.
- **Schrödinger’s cat (1935)** dramatised the puzzle of superposition; **decoherence** explains why we don’t see it in large objects.
- **Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle (1927)**: position and momentum can’t both be precisely known.
- **Quantum tunnelling** helps power the Sun and is used in **flash memory**.

=== why
Quantum mechanics is the foundation of chemistry and modern electronics, and it reveals that reality, at its smallest scales, works in ways that defy intuition. Understanding its basic ideas helps you make sense of technologies you use daily and separate genuine quantum science from the misleading way “quantum” is often used in marketing. The final lesson explores entanglement and the coming age of quantum technology.

##### LESSON
id: physics.quantum.2
title: Entanglement and the Quantum Future
minutes: 15
prereq: physics.quantum.1
check: In your own words: what is quantum entanglement, what did Bell’s experiments show, and why are quantum computers potentially powerful but still limited?

=== hook
In **1935**, Albert Einstein and two colleagues, **Boris Podolsky** and **Nathan Rosen**, published a paper arguing that quantum mechanics must be **incomplete**. They pointed to a strange consequence of the theory: two particles could be linked so that measuring one instantly seemed to determine the state of the other, however far apart they were. Einstein later called this “**spooky action at a distance**”.

Einstein believed there had to be hidden information inside the particles, deciding the outcomes in advance. For decades, the question seemed too philosophical to test. Then, in **2022**, the Nobel Prize in Physics went to **Alain Aspect**, **John Clauser** and **Anton Zeilinger** for experiments that settled it. Einstein, it turned out, was wrong: the spookiness is real.

=== core
**Quantum entanglement** occurs when two or more particles become linked so that their properties are correlated in a way that can’t be explained by each particle having its own independent, pre-set state.

For example, two entangled photons can be created so that if one is measured to have a certain polarisation, the other is always found to have a related polarisation, even if they’re on opposite sides of a laboratory, or hundreds of kilometres apart. Before measurement, neither has a definite polarisation; they exist in a shared superposition.

Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen (often abbreviated **EPR**) argued that this either meant faster-than-light influence, which seemed to clash with relativity, or that the particles carried **hidden variables**, pre-set instructions determining the results all along.

=== text Bell’s test
In **1964**, the Northern Irish physicist **John Stewart Bell** made a breakthrough. He showed that the hidden-variable idea and quantum mechanics make **different predictions**, which could be tested by measuring entangled particles at various angles and comparing the statistics.

If particles carried pre-set local instructions, the correlations between measurements would have to stay within a certain limit, now called **Bell’s inequality**. Quantum mechanics predicted the correlations would **exceed** that limit.

Experiments began in the 1970s, with John Clauser’s team, and were refined by Alain Aspect in France in the early **1980s** and Anton Zeilinger’s group in Austria, among others. In **2015**, several teams performed “loophole-free” tests closing the remaining gaps. Every time, the results matched quantum mechanics and **violated** Bell’s inequality. The world is not built from particles carrying simple local instructions.

=== callout analogy
Imagine two magic coins given to two friends who travel to different cities. Each flips their coin at the same moment, and whenever they compare results, they find their coins are linked in a pattern so strong that no pre-arranged plan, no secret script printed inside each coin, could ever reproduce it. Yet neither friend can control their own coin’s result, so they can’t use the coins to send a message. Entanglement is like that, though, as with all quantum analogies, the real thing is even stranger.

=== text No faster-than-light messages
If measuring one particle instantly affects the correlation with the other, can we use entanglement to send messages faster than light? **No**. Each person’s individual results look completely random. Only when the two sides **compare** their results, using ordinary communication limited by the speed of light, do the correlations appear. So entanglement doesn’t allow faster-than-light communication, and doesn’t break Einstein’s relativity in that sense, but it does show that nature is connected in ways classical physics never imagined.

=== text What is really going on? Interpretations
Quantum mechanics predicts experimental results with astonishing accuracy, but physicists still disagree about what it says about reality. Major **interpretations** include:

- **The Copenhagen interpretation**, associated with Niels Bohr: the wave function is a tool for predicting measurement outcomes; asking what happens between measurements may not have a meaningful answer. Measurement causes the wave function to “collapse” to a definite result.
- **The many-worlds interpretation**, proposed by **Hugh Everett** in **1957**: the wave function never collapses. Instead, every possible outcome happens, each in a separate, branching world. You only experience one branch.
- **Pilot-wave theory** (de Broglie–Bohm): particles always have definite positions, guided by a wave, but the guidance is non-local, connecting distant parts of the universe.
- Several other interpretations exist. They mostly make the **same predictions** for experiments so far, so choosing between them involves philosophy as much as physics.

=== steps How quantum computers work, and their limits
- Ordinary computers store information in **bits**, each either 0 or 1, as you saw in the Technology region.
- Quantum computers use **qubits**, which can be in a **superposition** of 0 and 1, and can be **entangled** with each other.
- Cleverly designed quantum algorithms use interference to make wrong answers cancel out and right answers reinforce, solving certain problems much faster than ordinary computers.
- Promising applications include simulating molecules for new materials and medicines, certain optimisation problems and, as you saw with **Shor’s algorithm**, breaking some of today’s encryption.
- But qubits are extremely fragile. Tiny disturbances from heat or vibration cause **decoherence**, destroying superpositions. Many quantum computers must be cooled to near **absolute zero**.
- In **2019**, Google claimed its quantum processor performed a specific calculation far faster than any classical supercomputer could, a milestone it called **quantum supremacy**; IBM disputed how large the advantage really was. Useful, error-corrected quantum computers that outperform classical machines on practical problems are widely expected to take years more of development.

=== text Quantum technology today
Quantum physics is already being used in new technologies beyond computing:

- **Quantum key distribution** uses quantum properties to share encryption keys so that any eavesdropping can be detected. In **2017**, Chinese scientists used the **Micius** satellite to demonstrate quantum-secured communication over more than 1,000 kilometres.
- **Quantum sensors** can measure time, gravity and magnetic fields with extraordinary precision, improving atomic clocks, navigation and medical imaging.
- Many countries are investing heavily. **India** launched a **National Quantum Mission** in **2023**, aiming to develop quantum computers, communication networks and sensors.

=== reveal Products are often marketed with words like “quantum healing” or “quantum energy”. Is that real physics?
Almost never. Genuine quantum effects like superposition and entanglement are extremely delicate and are quickly destroyed by interactions with a warm, noisy environment, such as the human body, except in carefully controlled situations. Marketing that uses “quantum” to sell health products, bracelets, water or wellness programmes typically borrows the word’s mystique without any connection to actual quantum physics. A good test: real quantum technologies come with specific, testable claims, published research and precise engineering. Vague promises that quantum energy will heal, balance or transform you are a warning sign, linking back to the scams and reasoning lessons.

=== keypoints
- **Entanglement** links particles so their properties are correlated beyond anything pre-set **hidden variables** could explain.
- **EPR (1935)** argued quantum mechanics was incomplete; Einstein called entanglement “**spooky action at a distance**”.
- **Bell (1964)** showed hidden variables and quantum mechanics make different predictions; experiments, finalised by **loophole-free tests in 2015**, support quantum mechanics (Nobel Prize **2022**).
- Entanglement **can’t** send faster-than-light messages.
- Interpretations include **Copenhagen**, **many-worlds** (Everett, 1957) and **pilot-wave** theory.
- **Quantum computers** use **qubits** in superposition and entanglement; they’re powerful for some problems but fragile and still developing.
- **Quantum key distribution**, **quantum sensors** and national programmes like **India’s National Quantum Mission (2023)** are emerging; “quantum” in health marketing is usually misleading.

=== why
Entanglement challenges our deepest assumptions about reality, locality and cause and effect, and it’s now being turned into real technology. Understanding what quantum physics does and doesn’t say helps you appreciate one of science’s most profound discoveries, follow developments in quantum computing and security, and see through the misuse of “quantum” in marketing.
