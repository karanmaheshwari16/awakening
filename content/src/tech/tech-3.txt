##### LESSON
id: tech.cryptography.2
title: Staying Secure in a Hostile Network
minutes: 15
prereq: tech.cryptography.1
check: In your own words: how do websites store passwords safely, why is two-factor authentication so effective, and why might quantum computers threaten today’s encryption?

=== hook
On **12 May 2017**, a piece of malicious software called **WannaCry** spread across the world in a matter of hours. It encrypted the files on infected computers and demanded payment in Bitcoin to unlock them. Around **200,000** computers in about **150 countries** were hit.

In the UK, the National Health Service was badly affected: thousands of appointments and operations were cancelled, and some ambulances were diverted. WannaCry spread using a weakness in older Windows systems. Microsoft had released a fix two months earlier. Many organisations simply hadn’t installed it. Most security disasters, it turns out, exploit ordinary gaps rather than genius hacking.

=== core
In the previous lesson, you saw that modern encryption is extremely strong. Attackers usually don’t try to break it. Instead, they target the weakest links: people, passwords, unpatched software and poorly protected data.

**Cybersecurity** is the practice of protecting systems, networks and data from attack. It’s often described in terms of three goals, sometimes called the **CIA triad**:

- **Confidentiality**: only authorised people can access information.
- **Integrity**: information isn’t altered without permission.
- **Availability**: systems and data are accessible when needed.

Attacks target one or more of these. WannaCry attacked availability by locking files; a data breach attacks confidentiality.

=== text How passwords should be stored
A well-run website never stores your actual password. Instead, it stores a **hash**: the output of a one-way mathematical function that turns any input into a fixed-length string of characters.

- The same password always produces the same hash, so when you log in, the site hashes what you typed and compares.
- But you can’t easily reverse a hash to get the password back.
- Good systems also add a random value called a **salt** to each password before hashing, so two people with the same password get different hashes, and use deliberately slow hash functions to make guessing expensive.

When a company is breached and its password database is stolen, attackers try to crack the hashes by guessing likely passwords at high speed. Weak, common passwords fall almost instantly. Lists of leaked passwords consistently show that millions of people use passwords like **123456**, **password** or **qwerty**.

=== text Credential stuffing: why reusing passwords is dangerous
Because people often reuse the same password across many sites, attackers take usernames and passwords leaked from one breach and automatically try them on other services, such as email, banking and shopping. This is called **credential stuffing**, and it’s one of the most common ways accounts are taken over.

The fix is to use a **different, strong password for every important account**. Since nobody can remember dozens of random passwords, security experts recommend a **password manager**: an app that generates and stores strong passwords, protected by one strong master password.

=== callout analogy
Reusing one password everywhere is like using the same key for your house, car, office and safe deposit box. If you lose it once, or a burglar copies it from one door, everything is open. A password manager is like a secure key cabinet holding a different key for each lock, which you open with one key you guard very carefully.

=== text Two-factor authentication
**Two-factor authentication (2FA)** requires two different kinds of proof to log in, usually from different categories:

- Something you **know**: a password or PIN.
- Something you **have**: your phone, a security key, a bank card.
- Something you **are**: a fingerprint or face.

Even if an attacker steals your password, they also need your phone or security key. Studies by Google and others have found that adding a second factor blocks the large majority of automated account takeover attempts. Codes from an authenticator app or a physical security key are generally more secure than codes sent by SMS, which can be intercepted through **SIM-swap** fraud, where criminals trick a mobile operator into transferring your number to their SIM card.

=== text The human element
Many attacks begin with **social engineering**: manipulating people rather than machines. **Phishing** emails and messages, covered in the scams lessons, trick people into entering passwords on fake sites or opening malicious attachments. Industry reports, such as Verizon’s annual data breach investigations, consistently find that a large share of breaches involve a human element: stolen credentials, phishing or simple mistakes.

**Ransomware**, like WannaCry, often gets in through a phishing email or an unpatched system, encrypts an organisation’s files and demands payment. Hospitals, schools, councils and businesses around the world have been targeted. Good **backups**, kept separately from the main systems, are one of the best defences.

=== steps Your personal security checklist
- **Use a unique, strong password** for every important account, ideally with a password manager.
- **Turn on two-factor authentication**, especially for email (which can reset all your other passwords), banking and social media.
- **Install updates promptly** on your phone, computer and apps. Many updates fix security weaknesses that attackers already know about.
- **Be suspicious of unexpected messages**, links and attachments, even from people you know, since their accounts may be compromised.
- **Back up important files** regularly to a separate location.
- **Lock your devices** with a PIN, password or biometrics, and enable remote wipe in case they’re lost.

=== text The quantum threat
Much of today’s public-key cryptography, including RSA, relies on mathematical problems that are extremely hard for ordinary computers, like factoring huge numbers. In **1994**, the mathematician **Peter Shor** showed that a sufficiently powerful **quantum computer**, which uses the strange rules of quantum physics that you’ll meet in the Physics region, could solve these problems efficiently.

No quantum computer today is anywhere near powerful enough. But because encrypted data stolen now could be decrypted later (“harvest now, decrypt later”), governments and companies are already preparing. In **August 2024**, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published its first standards for **post-quantum cryptography**: new methods designed to resist quantum attacks. Migrating the world’s systems will take many years.

=== reveal Should governments be able to read encrypted messages to catch criminals and terrorists?
This is one of the most contested debates in technology policy. Law enforcement agencies argue that end-to-end encryption lets criminals hide evidence, and some governments have proposed requiring a way for authorities to access messages with legal authorisation, sometimes called a “**backdoor**”. Many security experts respond that there’s no way to build a backdoor that only the “good guys” can use: any deliberate weakness could be found and exploited by criminals or hostile states, weakening security for everyone. Privacy advocates also warn about surveillance and misuse. Different countries have taken different positions, and the debate continues.

=== keypoints
- **Cybersecurity** protects **confidentiality**, **integrity** and **availability**.
- Websites should store **salted hashes**, not passwords; weak, common passwords are cracked quickly after breaches.
- **Credential stuffing** exploits reused passwords; use **unique passwords** and a **password manager**.
- **Two-factor authentication** blocks most automated takeovers; app codes and security keys beat SMS.
- Many breaches involve **people**: phishing, social engineering and mistakes; **ransomware** like **WannaCry (2017)** exploits unpatched systems.
- **Quantum computers** could break RSA (**Shor, 1994**); **post-quantum** standards were published in **2024**.

=== why
Your digital accounts hold your money, communications, photos and identity. A few simple habits, like unique passwords, two-factor authentication and prompt updates, protect you against most common attacks. Understanding security also helps you follow debates about privacy, surveillance and the future of encryption, which affect everyone online.

##### LESSON
id: tech.ai.1
title: How Machines Learn
minutes: 15
check: In your own words: what is the difference between traditional programming and machine learning, and how does a neural network learn from examples?

=== hook
In **March 2016**, in Seoul, the AI program **AlphaGo**, built by Google DeepMind, played five games of the ancient board game Go against **Lee Sedol**, one of the world’s strongest players. Go has more possible board positions than there are atoms in the observable universe, and experts had expected computers to need at least another decade to master it.

AlphaGo won **4–1**. In the second game, it played a move, **move 37**, so unusual that commentators thought it was a mistake. It turned out to be brilliant. AlphaGo hadn’t been programmed with rules for good Go strategy. It had **learned**, first from human games and then by playing millions of games against itself, and discovered ideas no human had taught it.

=== core
**Artificial intelligence (AI)** is a broad term for computer systems that perform tasks we normally associate with human intelligence: recognising images, understanding language, making decisions, playing games, translating and more.

The term was coined in **1956** at a summer workshop at **Dartmouth College** in the US, organised by **John McCarthy** and others, who optimistically suggested that significant progress could be made in a single summer. Six years earlier, in **1950**, Alan Turing had asked “Can machines think?” and proposed a test, now called the **Turing test**: could a machine hold a conversation indistinguishable from a human’s?

AI’s history has seen cycles of excitement and disappointment. Periods when funding and interest collapsed after progress stalled became known as **AI winters**, notably in the 1970s and late 1980s.

=== text Two ways to make a machine smart
**Traditional programming (rule-based AI)**: humans write explicit rules. For example, to detect spam: “if the email contains ‘free money’ and comes from an unknown sender, mark it as spam.” Early AI, called **symbolic AI** or **expert systems**, worked this way. It’s transparent, but brittle: the world is too messy for humans to write rules for everything. How would you write rules to recognise a cat in any photo?

**Machine learning**: instead of writing the rules, humans provide **examples**, and the computer finds the patterns itself. Show a system millions of emails labelled “spam” or “not spam”, and it learns which features predict spam, including patterns no human would have thought to write down.

Most of today’s AI successes come from machine learning.

=== callout analogy
Traditional programming is like teaching someone to cook by giving them a precise recipe. Machine learning is like letting someone taste thousands of dishes, telling them each time whether it’s good or bad, until they develop their own sense of what works, without ever being told the rules explicitly. They may end up a better cook than the recipe writer, but they may struggle to explain exactly why.

=== text Neural networks
The most powerful machine learning approach today uses **artificial neural networks**, loosely inspired by the brain.

A neural network is made of layers of simple units, often called neurons, connected to each other. Each connection has a **weight**: a number determining how strongly one neuron influences the next.

- The **input layer** receives data, such as the pixel values of an image.
- **Hidden layers** in the middle transform the data step by step. Each neuron combines its inputs, weighted by the connection strengths, and passes on a signal.
- The **output layer** produces the answer, such as “cat: 92% likely”.

Networks with many hidden layers are called **deep** neural networks, which is where the term **deep learning** comes from. Early layers in an image network might detect simple edges; later layers combine them into shapes, then into features like ears and whiskers, then into whole objects.

=== steps How a neural network learns
- Start with a network whose weights are set **randomly**. Its answers are essentially guesses.
- Show it a training example, such as a photo labelled “cat”.
- The network makes a prediction, say “dog: 60%”. Measure how **wrong** it was, using a mathematical measure called the **loss**.
- Work out how each weight contributed to the error, using a technique called **backpropagation**, and nudge every weight slightly in the direction that would have reduced the error. This process is called **gradient descent**.
- Repeat with millions of examples, many times over. Gradually, the weights settle into values that produce good predictions.
- Finally, test the network on examples it has **never seen**, to check it has learned general patterns rather than just memorising the training data.

=== text The deep learning revolution
Neural networks have existed since the 1950s and 1960s, and the key learning method, backpropagation, was popularised in **1986** by researchers including **Geoffrey Hinton**. But for decades, they were too slow and data-hungry to beat other methods.

Three things changed around 2010: huge **datasets** became available from the internet; **GPUs**, the graphics chips you met earlier, turned out to be ideal for neural network calculations; and researchers refined their techniques. In **2012**, a deep neural network called **AlexNet**, built by Hinton’s team at the University of Toronto, won the **ImageNet** image recognition competition by a large margin. That result triggered the explosion of deep learning that powers face recognition, voice assistants, translation, medical image analysis and much more today.

Hinton later shared the **2024 Nobel Prize in Physics** with John Hopfield for foundational work on neural networks.

=== reveal Can AI help with science, not just apps and games?
Yes, and one of the most striking examples is **protein folding**. Proteins are long chains of amino acids that fold into complex 3D shapes, and their shape determines what they do in the body. Working out a protein’s structure experimentally could take years. In **2020**, DeepMind’s **AlphaFold** predicted protein structures with accuracy comparable to experimental methods in many cases. Its predictions for hundreds of millions of proteins were made freely available to scientists worldwide, accelerating research in medicine and biology. **Demis Hassabis** and **John Jumper** of DeepMind shared the **2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry** for this work, along with **David Baker**, who designed new proteins computationally.

=== keypoints
- **AI** covers systems performing tasks associated with human intelligence; the term was coined at **Dartmouth in 1956**; **Turing** proposed his test in **1950**.
- **Rule-based** AI follows human-written rules; **machine learning** finds patterns from **examples**.
- **Neural networks** have layers of connected units with adjustable **weights**; **deep learning** uses many layers.
- Networks learn by measuring error (**loss**) and adjusting weights via **backpropagation** and **gradient descent**.
- **Big data**, **GPUs** and better techniques sparked the deep learning boom, marked by **AlexNet (2012)**.
- **AlphaGo** beat Lee Sedol in **2016**; **AlphaFold** transformed protein structure prediction (Nobel Prize, **2024**).

=== why
AI is already embedded in daily life, from your phone’s camera and keyboard to spam filters, maps and recommendations. Understanding how machines actually learn, from examples and adjustable weights rather than magic or consciousness, helps you judge both the hype and the fear. The next lesson looks at the most talked-about form of AI today: large language models, and their power and limits.

##### LESSON
id: tech.ai.2
title: Large Language Models: Power, Limits and Choices
minutes: 16
prereq: tech.ai.1
check: In your own words: how does a large language model generate text, why does it sometimes state false things confidently, and what is one major risk and one major benefit of this technology?

=== hook
In late **2022**, a chatbot called **ChatGPT** was released to the public. Within about two months, it was estimated to have reached **100 million** users, at the time one of the fastest adoptions of any consumer technology in history. People used it to write emails, explain homework, draft code, plan trips and argue about philosophy.

Large language models, the technology behind ChatGPT and similar systems from several companies, can produce remarkably fluent, useful text. They can also invent facts, references and quotes with complete confidence. Understanding how they work explains both their power and their failures, and helps you use them wisely.

=== core
A **large language model (LLM)** is a neural network trained on enormous amounts of text to predict what comes next.

At its core, the task is simple: given a sequence of text, predict the next small piece of text, called a **token** (often part of a word). Train a model to do this extremely well, across a huge portion of the text available on the internet and in books, and something surprising happens. To predict text accurately, the model has to absorb grammar, facts, styles of reasoning, common patterns of argument and much more.

When you ask an LLM a question, it generates a response **one token at a time**, each time choosing a likely next token based on everything before it, including your question and the words it has already written.

=== text The transformer breakthrough
Modern LLMs are built on an architecture called the **transformer**, introduced in a **2017** paper by researchers at Google titled “**Attention Is All You Need**”.

Its key innovation is a mechanism called **attention**, which lets the model weigh how relevant every word in a passage is to every other word, whatever their distance. In the sentence “The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because **it** was too big”, attention helps the model connect “it” to “trophy” rather than “suitcase”. Transformers can also be trained efficiently in parallel on GPUs, which allowed models to scale up to hundreds of billions of adjustable weights (often called **parameters**) and vast training datasets.

=== steps How a chatbot is made
- **Pre-training**: the model learns to predict the next token on a massive amount of text. This takes weeks or months on thousands of specialised chips, at enormous cost. The result can continue text, but isn’t yet a helpful assistant.
- **Fine-tuning**: the model is trained further on examples of helpful conversations, instructions and answers.
- **Learning from human feedback**: people rate or compare the model’s answers, and the model is adjusted to produce responses people judge as more helpful, honest and harmless. A widely used version of this is called **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)**.
- **Safety testing**: developers test for harmful behaviours and add safeguards, though no method is perfect.
- **Deployment**: the model is released, often with extra tools, such as the ability to search the web or run code, to compensate for its limitations.

=== callout analogy
Think of an LLM as someone who has read an astonishing amount, far more than any human could, and developed an excellent sense of how text usually continues. Ask them a question and they’ll produce a fluent, plausible answer instantly. Usually it’s right, because plausible and true often overlap. But when they don’t know, they don’t always say so; they produce something that **sounds** right. Fluency is not the same as knowledge.

=== text Hallucinations and other limits
LLMs have well-known weaknesses:

- **Hallucinations**: they can generate false information, such as invented facts, fake citations, non-existent court cases or wrong numbers, stated confidently. In a widely reported **2023** case in New York, lawyers were sanctioned after submitting a legal brief containing fake case citations generated by a chatbot. Hallucinations happen because the model generates plausible text, and doesn’t inherently check facts against a reliable source.
- **Bias**: trained on human-written text, models can reflect stereotypes and biases in that data, though developers work to reduce this.
- **Knowledge cut-offs**: a model’s built-in knowledge stops at its training date, unless it’s connected to search or other tools.
- **Reasoning errors**: they can make mistakes in arithmetic, logic and multi-step reasoning, though newer models have improved substantially.
- **Opacity**: even their creators can’t fully explain why a model produces a particular answer, an active research area called **interpretability**.

=== text Benefits and risks
LLMs offer real benefits: helping people learn, write and code; making information more accessible across languages, including Indian languages; assisting scientists, doctors and researchers; and automating tedious tasks.

They also raise serious concerns that are actively debated:

- **Misinformation and deepfakes**: AI can generate convincing fake text, images, audio and video at scale, with implications for scams, elections and trust.
- **Jobs**: AI may automate some tasks and transform many occupations. Economists disagree about how disruptive it will be and how quickly, and whether new jobs will offset losses, as happened in past technological shifts.
- **Copyright and consent**: many models were trained on text and images without their creators’ permission, leading to lawsuits and policy debates.
- **Energy and resources**: training and running large models consumes substantial electricity and water for cooling data centres.
- **Concentration of power**: building frontier models requires enormous resources, available to only a few companies and countries.
- **Safety and control**: some researchers worry about future, more capable systems behaving in ways their creators didn’t intend, a field known as **AI alignment**. Experts disagree strongly about how serious and how near these risks are.

=== text Rules for a new technology
Governments are beginning to respond. The **EU AI Act**, which entered into force in **2024**, classifies AI uses by risk level, banning some uses outright and imposing strict requirements on high-risk applications. Other countries, including the US, UK, China and India, have taken different approaches, ranging from voluntary commitments and guidelines to specific rules. How to encourage beneficial innovation while managing risks is one of the major policy questions of the decade.

=== steps Using AI tools wisely
- **Treat outputs as drafts, not facts.** Verify important claims, numbers and quotations with reliable sources.
- **Ask for sources**, then check that they exist and say what’s claimed.
- **Use it to learn, not to skip learning.** Asking an AI to explain a concept, quiz you or check your reasoning can help understanding. Copying answers can bypass the effortful retrieval that builds memory, as you saw in the Mind region.
- **Protect private information.** Avoid sharing sensitive personal, financial or confidential data unless you understand how it will be used.
- **Stay alert to fakes.** Be cautious about surprising images, audio or videos, especially ones that provoke strong emotions.

=== reveal Do large language models actually understand what they’re saying?
This is genuinely debated by researchers and philosophers. Some argue LLMs are sophisticated pattern-matchers with no real understanding, sometimes described as “**stochastic parrots**”, a phrase from a 2021 paper by Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru and colleagues. Others point to evidence that models build internal representations of concepts and can generalise in ways that look like understanding, at least in a functional sense. Part of the disagreement is about what “understanding” means in the first place, which links to the Philosophy region’s lessons on mind and consciousness. What is clear is that their behaviour can be impressively capable and surprisingly wrong, sometimes in the same answer.

=== keypoints
- An **LLM** is a neural network trained to predict the next **token**, producing text one token at a time.
- Modern LLMs use the **transformer** architecture (2017) and its **attention** mechanism.
- Chatbots are made through **pre-training**, **fine-tuning** and **learning from human feedback** (like **RLHF**).
- LLMs can **hallucinate**, reflect **bias**, have **knowledge cut-offs** and make **reasoning errors**.
- Benefits include learning, writing, coding and access; risks include **misinformation**, **job disruption**, **copyright**, **energy use** and **safety**.
- The **EU AI Act (2024)** is a major attempt at regulation; approaches differ worldwide.
- Use AI outputs as **drafts to verify**, not as unquestioned facts.

=== why
Large language models are becoming part of how people work, study and find information. Knowing how they generate text, and why they fail, lets you use them as powerful tools while avoiding their traps. It also prepares you to take part in some of the most important debates of your lifetime: how AI should be built, governed and shared.
