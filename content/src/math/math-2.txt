##### LESSON
id: math.probability.2
title: Conditional Probability and Bayes’ Theorem
minutes: 15
prereq: math.probability.1
check: In your own words: what is conditional probability, what is the prosecutor’s fallacy, and how does Bayes’ theorem help you update beliefs when new evidence arrives?

=== hook
In **1999**, an English solicitor named **Sally Clark** was convicted of murdering her two baby sons, who had died suddenly, weeks after birth, in 1996 and 1998. A key part of the case was testimony from a prominent paediatrician, who told the court that the chance of two babies in a family like hers both dying of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) was about **1 in 73 million**.

The figure was badly flawed. It wrongly assumed the two deaths were independent, ignoring genetic and environmental factors that could make a second SIDS death more likely in the same family. Worse, it confused the probability of the evidence with the probability of guilt. The Royal Statistical Society publicly criticised the statistics. Clark’s conviction was overturned in **2003**, after other evidence emerged, but she never recovered from the ordeal and died in 2007. Misunderstood probability can destroy lives.

=== core
In the previous lesson, you calculated probabilities of simple events. But in real life, probabilities often depend on **what you already know**. The probability that it will rain is different from the probability that it will rain **given that** the sky is full of dark clouds.

**Conditional probability** is the probability of an event **given** that another event has happened. It’s written P(A | B), read as “the probability of A given B”.

A crucial point: **P(A | B) is usually not the same as P(B | A)**.

- The probability that a person is **a cricketer, given that they’re Indian**, is small: most Indians aren’t professional cricketers.
- The probability that a person is **Indian, given that they’re a professional cricketer** on the Indian team, is essentially 100%.

Confusing these two directions is one of the most common and dangerous errors in reasoning.

=== text The prosecutor’s fallacy
The **prosecutor’s fallacy** is exactly this confusion, in a legal setting. It mixes up:

- The probability of the **evidence, if the defendant is innocent**: “If she were innocent, the chance of two such deaths would be tiny.”
- The probability that the defendant is **innocent, given the evidence**: “So the chance she’s innocent is tiny.”

These are not the same. Even if the evidence is very unlikely under innocence, it might also be very unlikely under guilt. In the Sally Clark case, double murder of infants by a mother is itself extremely rare. To judge guilt properly, the two unlikely explanations must be **compared** with each other, not judged in isolation.

A similar error happens with DNA evidence. If a DNA profile matches only 1 in a million people, that doesn’t mean there’s a 1 in a million chance the suspect is innocent. In a country of over a billion people, over a thousand people might match. The match is strong evidence, but its meaning depends on everything else known about the case.

=== callout analogy
Imagine you hear hoofbeats outside your window in Delhi. The probability of hearing hoofbeats, **if** there’s a zebra outside, is high. But the probability that there’s a zebra outside, **given** that you hear hoofbeats, is very low, because horses (and other hoofed animals) are far more common than zebras in Delhi. Doctors have a saying for this: when you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras.

=== text Bayes’ theorem
The mathematical tool for correctly reversing conditional probabilities is **Bayes’ theorem**, named after the English minister **Thomas Bayes**, whose work on it was published after his death in 1763. You met the core idea in the Philosophy region. In words, it says:

**Updated belief = prior belief × how much more likely the evidence is if the belief is true, compared with if it’s false.**

More formally, it combines three ingredients:

1. The **prior probability**: how likely the hypothesis was before seeing the evidence (the **base rate**).
2. The **likelihood**: how probable the evidence is if the hypothesis is true.
3. How probable the evidence is overall, including if the hypothesis is false.

=== steps Bayes in action with natural frequencies
- Psychologist **Gerd Gigerenzer** found that people reason much better with **natural frequencies**, counts of people, than with percentages. Here’s an example.
- A disease affects **1 in 100** people in a population. A screening test detects **90%** of people who have the disease. It also gives a false positive for **9%** of healthy people.
- Imagine **1,000** people. About **10** have the disease; the test correctly flags **9** of them.
- Of the **990** healthy people, 9% test positive anyway: about **89** false positives.
- So about **98** people test positive, but only **9** of them actually have the disease.
- The chance that a person who tests positive has the disease is 9 ÷ 98, about **9%**, not 90%.
- In one study, Gigerenzer found many doctors badly overestimated this probability when given percentages, but far more reasoned correctly when the same information was presented as natural frequencies.

=== text Updating beliefs step by step
One powerful feature of Bayesian reasoning is that it can be **repeated**. Each time new evidence arrives, yesterday’s updated belief becomes today’s prior.

Suppose the person in the example above tests positive (now about 9% likely to have the disease). If they take a **second**, independent test and it’s also positive, the probability rises much higher, because the false positives from the first test are now filtered again. This is why doctors often confirm screening results with further tests rather than acting on a single positive result.

This step-by-step updating describes how good scientists, detectives and forecasters ideally think: never fully certain, always adjusting confidence in proportion to the strength of new evidence.

=== reveal How do spam filters use Bayes’ theorem?
In **2002**, the programmer **Paul Graham** popularised a simple but effective approach to filtering spam email using Bayes’ theorem. The filter learns from emails you’ve marked as spam or not spam, recording how often each word appears in each. When a new email arrives, it combines the evidence from many words. Words like “lottery”, “winner” or “guaranteed” raise the probability that the email is spam; your friends’ names or work terms lower it. Each word updates the probability, just like adding new evidence. Such **naive Bayes** filters, so called because they simplify by treating each word as independent, worked remarkably well and influenced many email systems, though modern filters use more sophisticated machine learning, as you saw in the Technology region.

=== keypoints
- **Conditional probability** P(A | B) is the probability of A given that B is true; it’s usually **not equal** to P(B | A).
- The **prosecutor’s fallacy** confuses the probability of the evidence given innocence with the probability of innocence given the evidence, as in the **Sally Clark** case.
- **Bayes’ theorem** combines the **prior** (base rate), the **likelihood** of evidence and overall probability to update beliefs.
- With a rare disease, even an accurate test can produce mostly **false positives**; **natural frequencies** make this clearer (Gigerenzer).
- Beliefs can be **updated repeatedly** as new evidence arrives.
- **Spam filters** have used Bayesian reasoning to weigh evidence from many words.

=== why
Conditional probability and Bayes’ theorem help you interpret medical test results, legal evidence, news about risks and data of all kinds. They protect against some of the most consequential errors in reasoning, errors that have led to wrongful convictions and unnecessary medical treatments. They also offer a model for thinking well in general: start with what’s already likely, and update in proportion to the evidence.

##### LESSON
id: math.statistics.1
title: Reading Statistics Without Being Fooled
minutes: 15
check: In your own words: when is the median a better summary than the mean, what is sampling bias, and what does survivorship bias mean?

=== hook
In **1936**, an American magazine called *The Literary Digest* conducted one of the largest opinion polls ever attempted, mailing questionnaires to around **10 million** people and receiving about **2.4 million** replies. Based on this huge sample, it confidently predicted that the Republican candidate, **Alf Landon**, would defeat President **Franklin D. Roosevelt** in a landslide.

Roosevelt won one of the biggest landslides in American history, carrying 46 of 48 states. Meanwhile, a young pollster named **George Gallup**, using a much smaller but more carefully chosen sample, predicted the result correctly. The Digest’s mistake: its mailing lists came largely from telephone directories and car registration records, which, during the Great Depression, skewed towards wealthier people, who were more likely to vote Republican. A huge but biased sample gave a confidently wrong answer. The magazine soon went out of business.

=== core
**Statistics** is the science of collecting, summarising and interpreting data. It helps us make sense of large amounts of information and draw conclusions about the world from samples. It’s also easily misused, accidentally or deliberately.

Two of the most basic questions to ask about any set of data are:

1. **What’s typical?** (A measure of the centre, or average.)
2. **How spread out is it?** (A measure of variability.)

=== text Three kinds of average
- **Mean**: add up all the values and divide by how many there are. This is what most people call the average.
- **Median**: the middle value when the data are arranged in order. Half the values are above it, half below.
- **Mode**: the most common value.

These can give very different answers. Imagine ten people in a room, each earning **50,000** rupees a month. The mean and median are both 50,000. Now a billionaire walks in, earning **100 crore** rupees a month. The **mean** income jumps to over **9 crore** rupees, suggesting everyone in the room is extremely rich. The **median** stays at about **50,000**, a much better description of a typical person.

That’s why data on **incomes**, **house prices** and **wealth**, which are skewed by a few very large values, are usually better summarised by the **median**. When someone quotes an average, it’s worth asking: which average?

=== callout analogy
Think of the mean as a balancing point: if each value were a weight placed along a ruler, the mean is where the ruler balances. A single very heavy weight far out at one end shifts the balance point a long way. The median is simply the middle person in a queue sorted by height: it doesn’t care how tall the tallest person is, only who’s in the middle.

=== text Spread matters too
Two groups can have the same average but be very different. Two cities might both have an average annual temperature of 25 °C, but one might be pleasantly mild all year, while the other swings between freezing winters and scorching summers.

The **range** (highest minus lowest value) gives a rough idea of spread. A more useful measure, the **standard deviation**, describes how far values typically are from the mean. A small standard deviation means values cluster tightly around the average; a large one means they’re widely scattered.

Many natural measurements, like human height, roughly follow a **normal distribution**: a symmetrical, bell-shaped curve, with most values near the mean and fewer and fewer further away. In a normal distribution, about **68%** of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, and about **95%** within two.

=== text Samples and populations
We usually can’t measure everyone. So statisticians study a **sample** to learn about a whole **population**. The key to a good sample is that it’s **representative**: it reflects the population fairly.

The best way to achieve this is **random sampling**, where every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen. Surprisingly, a well-chosen random sample of around **1,000 to 2,000** people can estimate opinions in a country of hundreds of millions reasonably accurately, as long as it’s truly representative.

**Sampling bias** happens when some groups are more likely to be included than others, as with the Literary Digest poll. Online polls, phone-in surveys and social media polls are especially prone to bias, because the people who choose to respond are often different from those who don’t.

=== steps Survivorship bias: the missing planes
- During **World War II**, the US military studied bombers returning from missions over Europe, noting where they had been hit by enemy fire. The bullet holes clustered on the wings, the tail and the body.
- The obvious idea was to add armour where the holes were.
- The statistician **Abraham Wald**, working with the Statistical Research Group at Columbia University, pointed out a crucial flaw: the military was only looking at planes that had **survived** and returned.
- Planes hit in other places, such as the **engines**, didn’t make it back to be counted.
- Wald’s insight: the areas with **no** bullet holes on returning planes were where hits were most deadly. That’s where armour was most needed.
- **Survivorship bias** is everywhere: studying only successful companies, famous university dropouts who became billionaires, or old buildings that are still standing (because badly built ones collapsed long ago) can lead to badly misleading conclusions.

=== text Margins of error
Because a sample isn’t the whole population, estimates from samples come with uncertainty. Polls often report a **margin of error**, for example ±3 percentage points. This means that if the poll found 45% support for something, the true figure is likely, though not certain, to be between about 42% and 48%.

Margins of error only cover random sampling error. They don’t account for biases, like people refusing to answer, misunderstanding questions or not telling the truth, which can be larger.

=== reveal How can a graph be technically accurate but still misleading?
In several common ways. **Truncated axes**: starting the vertical axis at a value other than zero can make small differences look huge; a bar chart showing values of 50 and 52, with the axis starting at 49, makes one bar look three times taller. **Cherry-picked time periods**: choosing a starting point that makes a trend look better or worse. **Inconsistent scales**: uneven spacing of dates or values. **Missing context**: showing raw totals instead of per-person figures, so larger countries or cities look worse. **Dual axes**: plotting two different quantities on separate scales to suggest a link that isn’t there. When reading a graph, check the axes, the time period, the units and the source before drawing conclusions.

=== keypoints
- **Statistics** helps summarise data and draw conclusions from samples, but is easily misused.
- The **mean**, **median** and **mode** can differ; the **median** better describes skewed data like **incomes**.
- **Spread** matters: the **range** and **standard deviation** describe variability; many measurements follow a **normal distribution**.
- A **random, representative sample** of 1,000–2,000 people can estimate a large population’s views.
- The **Literary Digest poll (1936)** shows a huge but biased sample can be badly wrong.
- **Survivorship bias** (Wald’s bombers) comes from studying only the survivors.
- **Margins of error** cover only random error; **misleading graphs** often use truncated axes or cherry-picked periods.

=== why
Statistics shape news headlines, government policies, health advice, business decisions and political debates. Being able to ask “which average?”, “who was sampled?” and “what’s missing?” helps you see through misleading claims and make better decisions. The next lesson tackles the most important statistical trap of all: confusing correlation with causation.

##### LESSON
id: math.statistics.2
title: Correlation, Causation and Statistical Traps
minutes: 15
prereq: math.statistics.1
check: In your own words: why doesn’t correlation prove causation, what is Simpson’s paradox, and what does regression to the mean explain?

=== hook
For several years, the number of films starring the actor **Nicolas Cage** released each year in the United States rose and fell remarkably closely with the number of people who drowned by falling into swimming pools. When one went up, so did the other.

Obviously, Nicolas Cage films don’t cause drownings, and drownings don’t inspire film studios. The writer **Tyler Vigen** collected dozens of such **spurious correlations**, like the link between margarine consumption and divorce rates in one US state, to make a serious point with a joke: if you compare enough data sets, some will match by pure coincidence. Correlation, on its own, proves very little.

=== core
Two things are **correlated** when they tend to change together. A **positive correlation** means that when one increases, the other tends to increase too, like height and weight. A **negative correlation** means that when one increases, the other tends to decrease, like the price of something and how much people buy.

**Causation** means that one thing actually **causes** the other. Correlation can be a clue to causation, but it’s never proof on its own. When two things are correlated, there are several possible explanations:

1. **A causes B**.
2. **B causes A** (reverse causation).
3. **A third factor causes both** (confounding).
4. **Coincidence**.

=== text Confounding and reverse causation
**Confounding**: you met this in the Mind region’s nutrition lessons. Ice cream sales and drownings are correlated, not because ice cream causes drowning, but because **hot weather** increases both. The hidden third factor is called a **confounder**.

**Reverse causation**: sometimes the causal arrow runs the opposite way from what we assume. Studies once found that people who drank little alcohol seemed less healthy than moderate drinkers. Part of the explanation was that some non-drinkers had **stopped** drinking **because** they were already ill, so illness was causing low drinking, rather than low drinking causing illness. Later, more careful studies have cast doubt on the claimed health benefits of moderate drinking.

=== callout analogy
Imagine noticing that people who carry umbrellas are more likely to get wet. Does carrying an umbrella make you wet? No: rain causes both carrying umbrellas and getting wet. Or noticing that cities with more fire engines have more fires. Do fire engines cause fires? No: bigger cities have both more fires and more fire engines. Always ask what else might be going on.

=== text Establishing causation
How can scientists establish causation? The gold standard is the **randomised controlled trial (RCT)**, which you met in the nutrition lessons. Randomly assigning people to groups ensures that, on average, confounders are spread evenly, so differences in outcomes can be attributed to the treatment.

When experiments aren’t possible, such as studying whether smoking causes cancer (you can’t ethically force people to smoke), scientists combine many kinds of evidence. In **1965**, the British epidemiologist **Austin Bradford Hill** proposed considerations for judging causation from observational data, including: the **strength** of the association, **consistency** across different studies, **timing** (the cause must come before the effect), a **dose-response** relationship (more exposure, more effect) and a plausible **mechanism**. The case that smoking causes lung cancer satisfied all of these overwhelmingly.

=== steps Simpson’s paradox
- In **1973**, the University of California, Berkeley, was concerned about possible gender bias in graduate admissions. Overall, men applying had a noticeably higher admission rate than women.
- But when researchers looked at individual departments, they found that in most departments, women were admitted at **equal or higher** rates than men.
- How can both be true? Women had disproportionately applied to more **competitive** departments, which rejected most applicants of either sex. Men had applied more to departments with high admission rates.
- Combining all departments together created a misleading overall picture.
- This is **Simpson’s paradox**: a trend that appears in several groups of data can disappear or **reverse** when the groups are combined.
- The lesson: aggregated data can hide important structure. Always consider whether a lurking variable, like department choice, is distorting the overall pattern.

=== text Regression to the mean
When something is measured and an extreme result occurs, the next measurement tends to be closer to the average, simply by chance. This is **regression to the mean**.

Daniel Kahneman described a revealing example. Flight instructors in the Israeli Air Force believed that praising cadets after an excellent landing made them do worse next time, while criticising them after a poor landing made them improve. Kahneman pointed out that an excellent landing is partly luck, so the next one is likely to be closer to average, whatever the instructor says. Likewise, a terrible landing is likely to be followed by a better one. The instructors were seeing regression to the mean and mistaking it for the effect of their feedback.

Regression to the mean explains many apparent mysteries: the so-called “cover jinx” when athletes appearing on magazine covers after exceptional seasons then perform worse, and why people often seem to improve after trying a new remedy when they felt at their worst.

=== text Statistical significance and its misuse
Scientists often report whether a result is **statistically significant**, usually based on a **p-value**. Roughly, a p-value tells you how surprising the data would be **if** there were really no effect. A common threshold is p < 0.05.

But p-values are widely misunderstood:

- A p-value of 0.05 does **not** mean there’s a 95% chance the result is true.
- **Statistical significance** is not the same as **practical significance**. With a huge sample, a tiny, unimportant effect can be statistically significant.
- If researchers test many things and report only the significant ones, false positives are almost guaranteed, the problem of **p-hacking** you met in the Philosophy region.

Many statisticians now encourage focusing on the **size** of effects and the **uncertainty** around them, rather than a simple significant-or-not verdict.

=== reveal What happens when a number becomes a target?
It often stops being a good measure. This is **Goodhart’s law**, named after the British economist **Charles Goodhart**, often summarised as: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” If hospitals are judged on waiting times, some may find ways to game the figures rather than truly speed up care. If schools are judged only on exam results, teaching may narrow to the test. If police are judged on reported crime numbers, crimes may go unrecorded. The colonial rat bounty you met in the Economics region is an extreme example. Numbers are valuable tools, but when people are rewarded for hitting them, they often find ways to hit the number rather than achieve the goal behind it.

=== keypoints
- **Correlation** means two things change together; it doesn’t prove **causation**.
- Correlations can arise from **causation**, **reverse causation**, **confounding** or **coincidence** (spurious correlations).
- **Randomised controlled trials** best establish causation; **Bradford Hill’s considerations** help judge observational evidence.
- **Simpson’s paradox**: a trend in subgroups can reverse when data are combined, as in **Berkeley admissions (1973)**.
- **Regression to the mean**: extreme results tend to be followed by more average ones.
- A **p-value** below 0.05 doesn’t mean a 95% chance of truth; **statistical** isn’t the same as **practical** significance.
- **Goodhart’s law**: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

=== why
Every day, you encounter claims that one thing causes another: diets and health, policies and outcomes, products and success. Understanding correlation, confounding, Simpson’s paradox and regression to the mean protects you from some of the most common and persuasive errors in reasoning, and helps you judge evidence the way good scientists and decision-makers do.

##### LESSON
id: math.logic-proof.1
title: Logic: The Rules of Valid Reasoning
minutes: 14
check: In your own words: what is the difference between a conditional statement, its converse and its contrapositive, and why do so many people get the Wason selection task wrong?

=== hook
In **1966**, the British psychologist **Peter Wason** devised a simple puzzle. Four cards lie on a table. Each has a letter on one side and a number on the other. You can see: **E**, **K**, **4** and **7**. The rule is: “If a card has a vowel on one side, then it has an even number on the other.” Which cards must you turn over to check whether the rule is true?

Most people choose E and 4, or just E. The correct answer is **E and 7**. In many studies, only around **10%** of people solved it correctly. Yet when the same logic is framed as a familiar social rule, like checking whether people drinking alcohol are old enough, most people get it right. Our everyday reasoning, it seems, doesn’t always follow the rules of logic.

=== core
**Logic** is the study of valid reasoning: the rules that determine when a conclusion genuinely follows from premises. You met the basic ideas of arguments, validity and soundness in the Philosophy region. Mathematics relies on logic completely: every mathematical result must follow logically from earlier ones.

Logic works with **propositions**: statements that are either **true** or **false**, like “7 is a prime number” (true) or “all birds can fly” (false).

Propositions can be combined using logical connectives:

- **AND**: “A and B” is true only if both are true.
- **OR**: “A or B” is true if at least one is true. (In logic, “or” usually includes the possibility of both.)
- **NOT**: “not A” is true when A is false.

These are exactly the logic gates you met in the Technology region, where they’re built into computer circuits. The connection isn’t a coincidence: in **1854**, the English mathematician **George Boole** published *The Laws of Thought*, turning logic into a kind of algebra using true and false. A century later, his **Boolean algebra** became the foundation of digital computing.

=== text If-then statements
One of the most important logical forms is the **conditional** statement: “**If** P, **then** Q.” For example: “If it’s raining, then the ground is wet.”

A conditional statement makes a specific claim: whenever P is true, Q must be true. It doesn’t say anything about what happens when P is false.

From this one statement, we can form three related statements:

- **Converse**: “If Q, then P.” (“If the ground is wet, then it’s raining.”) This does **not** follow. The ground might be wet because someone watered the garden.
- **Inverse**: “If not P, then not Q.” (“If it’s not raining, the ground isn’t wet.”) This also does **not** follow, for the same reason.
- **Contrapositive**: “If not Q, then not P.” (“If the ground isn’t wet, then it isn’t raining.”) This **does** follow, and is logically equivalent to the original statement.

=== callout analogy
Think of the rule “If you’re in Mumbai, then you’re in India.” Being in India doesn’t mean you’re in Mumbai (the converse fails: you might be in Chennai). But if you’re **not** in India, you definitely aren’t in Mumbai (the contrapositive works). Picture a small circle (Mumbai) inside a big circle (India): being inside the small circle guarantees being inside the big one, but not the other way round.

=== text Two common logical errors
**Affirming the consequent**: “If P then Q. Q is true. Therefore P.” “If someone has the flu, they have a fever. She has a fever. So she has the flu.” Invalid: many things cause fever.

**Denying the antecedent**: “If P then Q. P is false. Therefore Q is false.” “If you study hard, you’ll pass. You didn’t study hard. So you won’t pass.” Invalid: you might still pass.

The two valid forms are:

- **Modus ponens**: “If P then Q. P is true. Therefore Q.”
- **Modus tollens**: “If P then Q. Q is false. Therefore P is false.”

=== steps Solving the Wason selection task
- The rule: “If a card has a **vowel** on one side, then it has an **even number** on the other.”
- **E** (a vowel): must be turned. If there’s an odd number on the back, the rule is broken.
- **K** (a consonant): doesn’t matter. The rule says nothing about consonants.
- **4** (even): doesn’t need turning. Even if there’s a consonant on the back, the rule isn’t broken; the rule doesn’t say only vowels have even numbers. Choosing 4 is affirming the consequent.
- **7** (odd): must be turned. If there’s a vowel on the back, the rule is broken. This uses the contrapositive: if not even, then not a vowel.
- Answer: **E and 7**. Now try a version people find easy: “If someone is drinking alcohol, they must be over 18.” Cards show: beer, cola, age 25, age 16. Most people instantly check the beer drinker and the 16-year-old, which is exactly the same logic.

=== text Logic across cultures
**Aristotle**, in the 4th century BCE, developed the first systematic study of logic in the West, focusing on **syllogisms**: arguments with two premises and a conclusion, like the Socrates example you met in the Philosophy region. His logic dominated Western thought for over two thousand years.

In India, the **Nyaya** school developed a sophisticated logic of its own. Its classic form of inference has **five steps**, illustrated by a famous example:

1. **Proposition**: there is fire on the hill.
2. **Reason**: because there is smoke on the hill.
3. **Example**: wherever there is smoke, there is fire, as in a kitchen.
4. **Application**: this hill has smoke, which is always accompanied by fire.
5. **Conclusion**: therefore, there is fire on the hill.

Including a concrete example made the reasoning persuasive in debate as well as valid. Buddhist logicians like **Dignaga** and **Dharmakirti** further developed theories of inference, and Chinese thinkers of the **Mohist** school also studied logic and argument.

=== reveal If logic is so important, why do people find the abstract card puzzle so hard but the drinking version easy?
Psychologists have debated this for decades. One influential explanation, from evolutionary psychologists **Leda Cosmides** and **John Tooby**, is that humans evolved specialised reasoning for **detecting cheaters** in social exchanges, since spotting people who take benefits without paying costs was crucial for survival in cooperative groups. So we reason well about rules involving permissions and obligations, but struggle with the same logic in abstract form. Others argue that familiarity and context matter more generally. Either way, the lesson is that our reasoning is shaped by context, and abstract logical thinking is a skill that improves with practice, which is exactly why mathematics and logic are worth studying.

=== keypoints
- **Logic** studies valid reasoning; it uses **propositions** that are true or false and connectives like **AND**, **OR** and **NOT**.
- **George Boole (1854)** turned logic into algebra, the foundation of **digital computing**.
- A **conditional** “if P then Q” is equivalent to its **contrapositive** (“if not Q then not P”), but not to its **converse** or **inverse**.
- **Affirming the consequent** and **denying the antecedent** are common invalid forms; **modus ponens** and **modus tollens** are valid.
- In the **Wason selection task**, only about **10%** solve the abstract version, but most solve social versions.
- **Aristotle’s syllogisms**, the **Nyaya five-step inference** and Buddhist and Mohist logic show logic developed in many cultures.

=== why
Logic underpins mathematics, computer science, law and clear thinking in everyday life. Recognising valid and invalid forms of reasoning helps you evaluate arguments, avoid being misled and express your own ideas more convincingly. The next lesson shows how mathematicians use logic to build proofs: arguments that establish truths with complete certainty.
